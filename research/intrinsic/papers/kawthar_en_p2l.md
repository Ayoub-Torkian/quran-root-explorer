## 5.20 Handling bad acts, not eliminating bad actors: the *dafʿ* strategy

Set the whole surah inside a single ethical frame and it clicks into place: the Qur'an's concern is to **handle the bad act**, not to **eliminate the bad actor**. Verse 3 is the proof. Confronted with a *hater*, the Prophet is **not** told to destroy, curse, or even answer him; he is told (verse 2) to **pray and sacrifice** — to turn to God in a good act — while the hater's being *cut off* is left to befall him **of itself** (by his own hatred, §5.19). The bad actor is neither attacked nor "removed"; the believer's whole assignment is the *good act* that meets the *bad act*. This is not an isolated posture but a stated strategy, with its own vocabulary.

**The verb is *dafʿ* — to repel, ward off — and it is preventive, not punitive.** "**Repel** (*idfaʿ*) [the bad] with that which is better; We know best what they allege" (23:96); "Good and evil are not equal. **Repel** [evil] with what is better — and behold, the one between whom and you there was enmity becomes as though a **warm friend**" (41:34) [TEXT]. The parallel formula uses the kindred verb *daraʾa*: the righteous "**avert** the bad deed (*sayyiʾa*) with the good deed (*ḥasana*)" (13:22; 28:54) [TEXT]. Two things are decisive here. First, what is repelled is the *sayyiʾa* — the **bad act**, the deed — not a person. Second, the aim is **conversion, not destruction**: the enemy is turned into a friend (41:34). The strategy handles the act and so *redeems* the actor; it does not seek to end him. And *dafʿ* is structurally **preventive**: "were it not for God's **repelling** (*dafʿ*) people by means of one another, the earth would be **corrupted**" (2:251), "monasteries, churches, synagogues and mosques would be **demolished**" (22:40) — repelling is what *stops* corruption from spreading, a warding-off before ruin, not a clean-up after [TEXT]. (The contrast the questioner draws with *rafʿ*, "lifting/removal," is worth a note: in the Qur'an *rafʿ* almost always means to **raise, elevate** — rank, the heavens, and, tellingly, "Did We not **raise high your renown**?" (94:4) — the very opposite of the hater's *abtar*, his cut-off name. So the live term for the strategy is *dafʿ*/*darʾ* — repel-and-convert — while *rafʿ* names the Prophet's elevation, not a reactive removal of foes.)

Note, too, the **economy of force**. Pure justice would permit a like-for-like: "the requital of an evil is an evil **like it** (*sayyiʾatun mithluhā*)" (42:40) — the same *mithl*-cap on evil we met in 6:160 (§5.12.1). But the Qur'an immediately lifts the gaze above the floor: "whoever pardons and **reconciles**, his reward is with God"; "whoever is patient and forgives — that is of the **steadiest of matters**" (42:40, 42:43) [TEXT]. The minimum is parity; the *better* is the good act that disarms the evil. Al-Kawthar sits exactly here: the Prophet is given the *better* response (worship), and the hater is left to the parity of his own deed.

### 5.20.1 Is *aḥsan* here a superlative? — and why not, strictly

The questioner asks the precise grammatical question: in *idfaʿ bi-llatī hiya **aḥsan*** — "repel with that which is *aḥsan*" — is *aḥsan* a **superlative** ("the best"), a **comparative** ("better"), or something else? The honest answer, on reflection and on the corpus evidence, is **"something else": *aḥsan* here is best taken as the *absolute* elative — "the good in its finest, essential form" — not as a rung on a *ḥasan → aḥsan* scale of degrees at all.** (An earlier draft of this study called it a plain comparative; that was too narrow, and we correct it here.)

The key is that Arabic's *afʿal* form (*aḥsan*, from *ḥ-s-n*) has **three** uses, not two:

- **Comparative** ("better than"), marked by a following ***min*** ("better *than* X").
- **Superlative** ("the best of"), marked by the **definite article** (*al-aḥsan*) or a defining construct (*aḥsanu l-…* — as in *aḥsanu l-khāliqīn*, "the best of creators," 23:14; 37:125; *aḥsana l-ḥadīth*, "the best discourse," 39:23).
- **Absolute / intensive** (the grammarians' *afʿal* that is *not* of *tafḍīl*): the form denotes the quality **in its pure, perfect form**, with no real ranking implied.

That third use is unmistakable in the Qurʾān, and it is what disarms the "superlative vs comparative" dichotomy. Consider "**who is better than God** (*man aḥsanu mina-llāhi*) in judgement?" (5:50), "**who, than God,** is better in colouring?" (2:138), "**who is truer than God** (*aṣdaqu mina-llāhi*) in speech?" (4:122). These have the comparative *min*, yet plainly do **not** place God a notch above rivals on a shared scale of goodness; they assert God's goodness/judgement as **absolute** — there is simply *no* other term in the comparison. The same is true of the rhetorical "**who has a better word** (*aḥsanu qawlan*) than one who calls to God?" (41:33) and "a better religion?" (4:125): the form is elative, but the sense is "this is *the* good — calling to God is good *as such*," not "marginally more good than other good speech." Here *aḥsan* is **not the superlative of *ḥasan*; it is absolute good in its best form** — exactly the questioner's point.

Now place *bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* in that light. It is a **fixed Qurʾānic formula** — "argue in the way that is *aḥsan*" (16:125; 29:46), "speak in the way that is *aḥsan*" (17:53), "repel with the way that is *aḥsan*" (23:96; 41:34) [MEASURED: five occurrences]. In none of these is there a *min* fixing a comparison, nor an *al-* fixing a superlative; the elative stands bare and reads as the **absolute good manner** — the finest, good-in-itself way to argue, to speak, to repel. So *idfaʿ bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* does **not** mean merely "respond with something a shade better than the evil"; it means "respond **with the (absolute) good** — the finest response there is." The "the best" of the translations is therefore right in *force* — not because the word is a marked morphological superlative (it is not; the article/construct is absent), but because it is the **absolute elative**, good-in-its-perfection. And this fits the surah exactly: the response to the gift (prayer, sacrifice) is not "a bit better than hatred"; it is **the good as such**, offered God-ward, against which the *sayyiʾa* is simply not on the same scale (41:34: "the good and the bad are **not equal**").

We mark the confidence. **[MEASURED]:** *dafʿ*/*darʾ* repelling the *sayyiʾa* with the *ḥasana*/*aḥsan* (23:96; 41:34; 13:22; 28:54); the enemy-to-friend outcome (41:34); *dafʿ* as preventive (2:251; 22:40); the *mithl*-cap on evil with the higher call to pardon (42:40, 42:43); *rafʿ* = "raise/elevate," incl. 94:4 "raised your renown"; and the wording *allatī hiya aḥsan* lacking both *al-* and *min*. **[INFERRED]:** the framing "handle the bad act, redeem (don't eliminate) the bad actor," and that al-Kawthar enacts it (worship in answer to hatred; the hater self-severs); and the grammatical verdict that *aḥsan* here is the **absolute / intensive** elative — "the good in its finest form" — rather than either a marked superlative (no *al-*/construct) or a mere comparative on the *ḥasan* scale; the corpus proof is the "*who is better than God?*" construction (2:138; 5:50; 4:122), where the elative is plainly absolute, and the fixed formula *bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* (16:125; 17:53; 29:46; 23:96; 41:34). The frame is secure: the surah meets a *bad act* with **the good as such** — not a marginal improvement on the evil — and lets the *bad actor*'s fate be his own.
