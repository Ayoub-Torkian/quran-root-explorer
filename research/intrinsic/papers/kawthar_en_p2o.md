## 5.20.2 "Follow the best of the word" (39:18): must one first audit the bad?

The verse *yastamiʿūna l-qawla fa-yattabiʿūna aḥsanahu* — "they listen to the word and follow the best of it" (39:18) — is often read as praise of open, discerning listening: *hear all speech, including the bad, then select the best.* The questioner proposes the opposite: if *aḥsan* is **absolute good** (§5.20.1), then "follow the best of it" need not mean surveying evil to find a relative best, and 41:33 ("who has a better word than one who calls to God?") shows the absolute-good word is identifiable on its own — so the believer goes straight to it, with no obligation to ingest bad arguments. There is a strong internal case for this, and an honest counter-case.

**The internal case (for the questioner).** Read in its own sūra, *al-qawl* in 39:18 is most naturally **the divine word**, not "speech in general." Two verses later the same sūra says God "has sent down the **best discourse** (*aḥsana l-ḥadīth*)" — the Qurʾān itself (39:23) — and later it commands, "**follow the best of what has been sent down to you from your Lord**" (*ittabiʿū aḥsana mā unzila ilaykum*, 39:55) [TEXT]. So within Sūra 39, "follow the *aḥsan*" is applied to the **contents of revelation** — follow its highest — not to a mixture of good and bad speech. The framing confirms it: the people praised have *already* "shunned ṭāghūt" (false gods) and turned to God (39:17); they are not auditing falsehood, they have rejected it. And 41:33 identifies the absolute-good word as **the call to God**, which such servants follow directly. The wider Qurʾān even warns against indiscriminate intake: "do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge — hearing, sight, and heart, all are accountable" (17:36) [TEXT]. On this reading, 39:18 praises *attending to the divine word and following its absolute best*, and there is no duty to expose oneself to evil speech to locate the good.

**The counter-case (against over-reading it).** Two cautions, in the spirit of §7. First, a **grammatical** one that cuts against the clean "absolute" claim: in *aḥsana-**hu*** the suffix "-*hu*" ("of **it**") is **partitive** — "the best **of** the [speech]" — which is a *within-a-set* superlative, not the bare absolute elative of *bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* (23:96). So 39:18's *aḥsan* is, if anything, *more* relative/superlative than absolute; importing the pure-absolute reading here is a slight category-slip. Second, an **exegetical** one: the discerning-listening reading ("weigh views, follow the best") is grammatically available (*yastamiʿūna l-qawla* can be generic "speech") and is held by reputable commentators; 41:33 does not **refute** it — it only **removes its necessity**, by showing the good is independently recognizable. One can listen broadly *and* recognize the call to God as best; discernment does not require that the good be defined only by contrast with surveyed evil.

**Calibrated verdict.** The questioner's conclusion — that the believer need not audit bad arguments, because the absolute-good word is identifiable as such — is **defensible and arguably the better reading** in context (the divine *al-qawl* of 39:23/39:55; the prior shunning of ṭāghūt; the accountability of 17:36). But it is **not proven**, and its strongest lever (the absolute elative) is **weaker here** than in 23:96, because "-*hu*" makes 39:18 partitive ("the best *of it*"). So: **[INFERRED, moderate]** that 39:18 does not *mandate* ingesting evil speech, with the divine-word chain (39:18 ↔ 39:23 ↔ 39:55 ↔ 41:33) as real support; **[conceded]** that a discerning-listening reading remains legitimate, and that the grammar of *aḥsanahu* is partitive, not purely absolute. The most we should claim is that the Qurʾān **does not require** auditing falsehood and points the servant toward the recognizable absolute good — not that it forbids discernment among speech.
