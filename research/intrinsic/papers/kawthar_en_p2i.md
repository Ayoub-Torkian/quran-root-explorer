## 5.17 How wide are "pray" and "sacrifice"? What the corpus licenses — and what it does not

The user's question is exactly the right one to put to a self-interpreting text: when verse 2 says *ṣalli* ("pray") and *inḥar* ("sacrifice"), is each word the **narrow ritual** — the five-times prayer, the slaughter of a camel — or a **more comprehensive** act of which the ritual is only the instituted form? The method answers by reading each root's full dataset. Instructively, the two words come back with *opposite* verdicts, and the difference is itself a lesson in honest interpretation.

**"Pray" (*ṣ-l-w*): the corpus does license a wider sense.** Across its 90 verses, *ṣalāt* is, dominantly, the ritual prayer — by a cue count, about 71 of 90 verses [MEASURED]. But a clear minority opens the root onto something larger. God and His angels *yuṣallūna* upon the Prophet (33:56), and "it is He who *yuṣallī* upon you, and His angels" (33:43) — here the root cannot mean ritual prayer (God does not perform prayers); it means to **turn toward with blessing, to connect, to send grace**. The Prophet is told to *ṣalli ʿalayhim*, "bless them," over those who give in charity (9:103); the righteous receive *ṣalawāt* — "blessings" — from their Lord (2:157) [TEXT]. So the root's underlying sense is a **turning-toward, a living connection** between Lord and servant, with grace flowing in both directions; the ritual prayer is the **instituted form** of that turning, not its whole meaning. This bears directly on verse 2, whose wording is *ṣalli li-rabbi-ka* — "turn in prayer **to your Lord**." The explicit direction (*li-* "to") foregrounds exactly the relational, connective sense the corpus attests: the response to the gift is the **whole self turning toward the Giver**, of which standing in prayer is the embodiment. The ritual and the comprehensive are not rivals here; the corpus lets us read the ritual as the *form* and the turning-to-the-Lord as the *substance*. This wider reading is **internally grounded** (33:43; 33:56; 9:103; 2:157), so we affirm it, with moderate confidence.

**"Sacrifice" (*n-ḥ-r*): the corpus licenses only the devotional-offering sense — and we must stop there.** Here discipline pulls the other way. *Naḥr* is a *hapax*; it has no second Qur'anic occurrence to widen it, so its meaning can be drawn **only** from its semantic neighbours, and those neighbours speak with one voice: **sacrifice**. The Qur'an's sacrificial vocabulary surrounds it — the *budn*, the "sacrificial camels… among the symbols of God" over which His name is pronounced (22:36); the "rite" (*mansak*) appointed for every nation (22:34); and, decisively for the *meaning*, "it is not their flesh or their blood that reaches God, but your **piety** (*taqwā*)" (22:37); and the summary dedication, "my prayer and my **rite** (*nusuk*)… are for God" (6:162) [TEXT]. From the corpus, then, *inḥar* is the **devotional offering** — an act of giving-back to the Lord whose true substance is piety, not meat. That much is internally secure.

But the root *n-ḥ-r* also carries, in the **lexicon**, senses beyond the Qur'an's attested usage — the *naḥr* as the upper chest/base of the throat (the spot where a camel is stabbed), and from it the ideas of *facing*, *turning the chest toward*, even (in some later interpretations) raising the hands to the chest in prayer. It is tempting to fold these into a grand reading — "turn your whole breast to the Lord." **The method forbids us from asserting it.** These senses are *external* to the Qur'an's own usage: the root appears once, and where the text itself supplies context, that context is sacrifice (22:34–37), not posture or direction. (We also keep *naḥr*, *n-ḥ-r* with *ḥāʾ*, distinct from *nahār*, "daytime," which is *n-h-r* with *hāʾ* — a different root entirely.) By the One Law, the chest/facing reading is a **lexical possibility, corroborative at most, not established evidence**; we record it, flagged, at low confidence, and do **not** build on it.

**The latent link, and its limit.** The user is right that the link is latent and must be cross-referenced — and the cross-referencing yields a real result for *ṣalāt* (its connective sense is explicit elsewhere, 33:43/56) and a deliberately bounded one for *naḥr* (its sacrificial sense is explicit in 22:34–37; its wider lexical senses are not). Read together within those limits, *ṣalli li-rabbika wa-nḥar* is the **whole self turning to the Giver (prayer-as-connection) and giving back to Him (offering-as-piety)** — orientation and offering, the pair that 6:162 gathers as "my prayer and my rite… for God." That is the comprehensive devotional response the gift requires, stated in the surah's own two words and confirmed, each to its proper extent, by the rest of the text.

We mark the confidence. **[MEASURED]:** the *ṣalāt* sense-distribution (≈71/90 ritual; the blessing/connection uses at 33:43, 33:56, 9:103, 2:157); the directional *li-rabbika*; and *naḥr*'s sole internal anchors in the sacrifice field (22:34, 22:36, 22:37; 6:162). **[INFERRED, affirmed]:** that *ṣalāt* in 108:2 carries the comprehensive "turning-to-the-Lord" sense (grounded in the attested connective uses; moderate confidence). **[INFERRED, internally grounded]:** that *naḥr* is devotional offering whose substance is *taqwā* (strong). **[REPORT / lexical, flagged, not internally established]:** the wider "chest/facing/posture" senses of *n-ḥ-r* — recorded for completeness, demoted by the One Law, and not used as a basis for any claim. The contrast between the two words is the section's real lesson: the same method that *expands* "pray" (because the corpus warrants it) *restrains* "sacrifice" (because the corpus does not) — coverage and honesty in the same breath.
