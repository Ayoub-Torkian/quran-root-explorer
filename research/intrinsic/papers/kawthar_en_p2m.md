## 5.21 One hater, unnamed — and Abu Lahab, named: principle and instance

*Shāniʾaka* is **singular** — "your hater," one person — not the plural "haters," and the man is left **unnamed**; the case is latent. Internally the text says nothing of his identity, and the external naming-traditions are multiple and unstable (§3.1), hence inadmissible. Two internal observations follow.

First, the **singular keeps the focus on a self's stance, not a faction.** The Qur'an's usual word for opposition is plural and sociological — the *aʿdāʾ*, "enemies," of God and the faithful (2:98; 60:1). A plural here would have turned verse 3 into a verdict on a *group*. The singular *shāniʾ* turns it instead on **one heart's posture** — hatred — and, being unnamed, on the **type** rather than a person: *whoever* takes that stance is the *abtar*. The verdict is a principle for a role, not a note about one Meccan. This is consistent with the surah's whole centre of gravity: the **self and its transformation** is the anchor; the adversary's identity is peripheral, which is precisely why the text can leave it blank.

Second, the silence is a **choice**, and the Qur'an's own practice proves it — in the neighbouring Sūrat al-Masad (111), where an opponent **is** named: "**Perish** the hands of **Abū Lahab**, and perish he" (111:1). Yet even there the "name" is an **epithet** — *Abū Lahab*, "father of flame" — a characterizing term sealed by his fate two verses later, "he will enter a **flaming fire** (*nāran dhāta lahab*)" (111:3): the same root *l-h-b*, the man named by what consumes him [TEXT]. So the Qur'an, when it identifies an opponent, identifies him **diagnostically** — Abū Lahab by his fieriness, the *shāniʾ* by his hatred — never by a neutral proper name. That 108 withholds even the epithet, where 111 supplies one, shows 108's anonymity to be deliberate **generalization**, not a gap.

The parallel pays a further dividend. Al-Masad seals its named man with *tabba* — "ruin, loss, perishing" (root *t-b-b*) — a fate adjacent to *abtar*'s severance; and it adds the line that ties him to this study's economy of grace: "**neither his wealth availed him, nor what he had earned** (*mā kasab*)" (111:2) [TEXT]. The opponent's *amassing* — the *takāthur* pole, the "earned" piled up for the self (§5.12) — saves nothing; he is left, like the *abtar*, with nothing that continues. So the two short surahs read as **principle and instance**: 108 states the law (the hating self severs itself — *abtar*), while 111 shows it realised in one named self (Abū Lahab perishes — *tabba* — his earnings void). Both keep the self's stance, not the actor's identity, at the centre; 108 abstracts it to anyone, 111 fixes it on one — and even there only to seal the *stance*, not to dignify the *person*.

We mark the confidence. **[MEASURED]:** *shāniʾaka* is singular; the Qur'an's enemies are usually plural (2:98; 60:1); Sūra 111 names by the epithet *Abū Lahab*, matched to *lahab* "flame" (111:1, 111:3), seals him with *tabba* (root *t-b-b*), and voids "what he earned" (*kasab*, 111:2). **[REPORT], bracketed:** all identifications of the 108 hater (and the equation of *Abū Lahab* with a specific historical person) — uncertain transmissions, not used as evidence. **[INFERRED]:** that the singular-and-unnamed *shāniʾ* generalises the verdict to the *type* (any hating self), that 111's naming-by-epithet shows 108's silence to be a deliberate generalisation, and that the two surahs stand as principle and instance. The reading keeps faith with the anchor: it is the **self's stance** — hatred, amassing — that severs, whoever the self happens to be.
