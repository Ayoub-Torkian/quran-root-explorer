## 5.25 The map of address: the second person (*-ka* and *-ta*)

The surah is, grammatically, a direct address to one person. Three of its ten words carry the second-person masculine singular pronoun *-ka* ("you / your"): *aʿṭaynā**ka*** ("We gave **you**", v1), *li-rabbi**ka*** ("to **your** Lord", v2), and *shāniʾa**ka*** ("**your** hater", v3). It is worth asking, with the corpus as the measure, where this places al-Kawthar among the passages that address the Prophet — and the result is unusually clean.

**A measured axis: personal address versus the prophetic office.** Address to the Prophet divides internally into two registers. One is *personal*: God speaking to the man about his own state, gift, grief, or vindication — marked by a high density of the *-ka* pronoun and, as we will see, by the **absence** of the transmission-imperative *qul* ("say!"). The other is the *office*: God commissioning the messenger-function — *qul*, *balligh* ("convey"), the legislative *yasʾalūnaka* ("they ask **you**… say…") — marked by *qul*. Counting word-final *-ka* per hundred (basmala-excluded) tokens across all 114 sūras places **al-Kawthar first in the entire Qurʾān** at 30.0% (three of ten content tokens), followed immediately by al-Sharḥ (94) at 29.6% and al-Ḍuḥā (93) at 22.5%; the rest of the corpus falls away sharply (the next, al-Infiṭār, is 11.1%, and the bulk sit below 6%). All three of these top sūras carry ***qul* = 0** [MEASURED]. The *qul*-heavy sūras — al-Isrāʾ (14 occurrences), al-Raʿd (10), the four *qul*-openers 109/112/113/114 — sit low on *-ka* density. The two markers are, across these surfaces, nearly anti-correlated: where the address is most personal it is least an office-instruction, and vice versa ({{F11}}). By this measure al-Kawthar is the **most personally-addressed sūra in the Qurʾān**, squarely on the individual side, with none of the transmission marker. (Caveat: word-final *-ka* is in general referent-blind — it also attaches to Moses, Adam, the disbelievers — so the corpus-wide count is a proxy; but for the three top sūras every *-ka* token was inspected and is second-person to the Prophet, so the placement at the top is secure. [MEASURED, with the proxy flagged.])

**The vocative summons — and al-Kawthar's pointed lack of it.** The most explicit way the Qurʾān addresses the Prophet is the vocative *yā ayyuhā* ("O…!"), and it comes in two keys. By **office / title**: *yā ayyuhā l-nabī* ("O Prophet!", 13×: 8:64, 65, 70; 9:73; 33:1, 28, 45, 50, 59; 60:12; 65:1; 66:1, 9) and *yā ayyuhā l-rasūl* ("O Messenger!", 2×: 5:41; 5:67) — these summon him by his *function*, and overwhelmingly introduce community-facing or legislative instruction (the wives, warfare, the hypocrites, divorce, and *"convey what was sent down to you"*, 5:67): the office register again, parallel to the communal *yā ayyuhā lladhīna āmanū* ("O you who believe", 89×) and *yā ayyuhā l-nās* ("O mankind", 20×). By **intimate epithet**: *yā ayyuhā l-muzzammil* ("O you wrapped one!", 73:1) and *yā ayyuhā l-muddaththir* ("O you cloaked one!", 74:1) — addressing him in his human, physical state at the tender opening of the mission: the personal register. **Al-Kawthar uses neither** [MEASURED]. It opens with no summons at all — not "O Prophet," not "O wrapped one" — but the bare declarative *innā aʿṭaynāka*, "We gave you." This is the most *unmediated* address on the gradient: where the office is hailed by title and even the intimate call names an epithet, the gift is simply *handed over* in the second person, with no vocative needed. It is one more measure of how personal this sūra's address is — God does not *call* the Prophet before giving; He gives [INFERRED, ~80%].

**Is *qul* personal — or a prescription to the people through the Prophet?** This is the right refinement, and it corrects the axis itself. *Qul* ("Say!") is, grammatically, a second-person singular imperative *to the Prophet* — so it is, formally, personal address: God commands *him*. But its *function* is transmission — what follows *qul* is to be conveyed to an audience; the Prophet is the conduit and the content is for the people. *Qul* is thus the **hinge** between the registers, and it shows that the true axis is not "personal vs impersonal" but the **terminus** of the address: does it *pass through* the Prophet to others (*qul…*, *balligh…*, *yasʾalūnaka… qul…*), or does it *rest on him* (his gift, his Lord, his hater, his own worship)? On that axis al-Kawthar is maximally **self-terminating**: it has no *qul* at all, and its two imperatives — *ṣalli*, *nḥar* — are acts the Prophet *performs*, not words he *relays*. Contrast the *qul*-sūras (109, 112, 113, 114), whose very content is speech to be spoken outward, and even the cognate *daʿwa* command *udʿu ilā sabīli rabbika* ("call to the way of your Lord", 16:125; §5.20.3), which is transmissive by definition. In al-Kawthar the gift received, the Lord turned to, the hater turned back, and the worship commanded all terminate in the Prophet himself — which is why the sūra reads as God speaking *to* him, not *through* him [MEASURED for *qul* = 0 and for the self-act content of the imperatives; INFERRED for the terminus framing, ~85%].

**The gift-verb roster confirms the same cluster.** Restricting to the rare verbs of *giving* and asking which place the Prophet as the direct second-person recipient: *aʿṭā* (root ʿ-ṭ-w) does so in only **93:5** (*yuʿṭīka rabbuka*, "your Lord will give you") and **108:1** (*aʿṭaynāka*) — out of its twelve occurrences; *sharaḥa* ("expand, relieve") does so only in **94:1** (*a-lam nashraḥ laka ṣadrak*) — out of five; and *wahaba* ("bestow", chiefly of progeny) does so **never to the Prophet** across its twenty-two occurrences, where the recipients are Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, Zachariah→Yaḥyā, Mary→Jesus, David→Solomon, and the supplicants of duʿāʾ [MEASURED]. So the personal *divine gift to the Prophet* is, lexically, the 93 / 94 / 108 triad — and it pointedly avoids *wahaba*, the verb of a specific bestowed child, which underwrites the open-referent reading argued in §5.11 (the gift is named with the unbounded *aʿṭā* + the genus-noun *kawthar*, not with the verb that would fix it to a particular descendant).

**Two on the self, one on the adversary.** Within this register a further distinction separates al-Kawthar from its two neighbours. In al-Ḍuḥā every *-ka* token is self-directed (*waddaʿaka, rabbuka, laka, yuʿṭīka, yajidka, wajadaka, rabbuka* — your Lord, your state, your gift); in al-Sharḥ likewise (*laka, ṣadraka, ʿanka, wizraka, ẓahraka, dhikraka, rabbuka* — your breast, your burden, your renown). Both are *pure* personal consolation. Al-Kawthar alone turns the second person **outward onto the adversary**: *shāniʾaka*, "**your** hater" (v3), whom verse 3 then reverses into *al-abtar* [MEASURED]. So the surah's spine is *two-self-plus-one-enemy-of-self*: what the Prophet is given, to whom he turns, and who hates him — gift, response, and vindication, all pinned to the one addressee. It is personal address that also names, and answers, the personal enemy.

**"You" is not "for you": the grammar of the gift.** A finer distinction sharpens the contrast, because the second person comes in functionally different forms: the bare *-ka* as a verb's **object** ("gave *you*"), the *-ka* as a noun's **possessive** ("*your* Lord", "*your* hater"), and the preposition-plus-*-ka* **benefactive** *laka* (لك, "*for* you / in your favour") — and they say different things. Measured across the cluster, al-Sharḥ is *benefactive-rich* — God acts *for* and *on behalf of* the Prophet: "did We not expand *for you* (*laka*) your breast… and lift *from you* (*ʿanka*) your burden… and raise *for you* (*laka*) your renown?" (94:1–4) [TEXT]. Al-Ḍuḥā has one such benefactive ("the Hereafter is better *for you*", *laka*, 93:4). **Al-Kawthar has none**: its three second persons are one *object* (*aʿṭaynāka*, the gift received) and two *possessives* (*li-rabbika*, "your Lord", with the لـ meaning "to"; *shāniʾaka*, "your hater") — and no benefactive *laka* at all [MEASURED]. The interpretive payoff is real: al-Sharḥ and al-Ḍuḥā are *consolation done **for** him* (relief, lifting, raising — the Prophet as beneficiary), whereas al-Kawthar is *a gift **given to** him to which he must respond* (he receives the object, he turns to **his** Lord, he faces **his** hater). This is consistent with al-Kawthar alone carrying the imperatives *ṣalli* and *nḥar*: it is not pure comfort *for* the Prophet but a gift *to* him that summons a return — a difference the *laka*/*-ka* grammar makes visible [INFERRED, ~80%]. (The corpus-wide density count above does not separate these functions; a function-tagged second-person index is noted in §10.)

**The verb-suffix *-ta*, and what its absence tells us.** The second person is carried not only by the pronoun *-ka* but by the perfect-verb agreement suffix *-ta* ("you did"): *adhinta* ("you permitted", 9:43 *ʿafā-llāhu ʿanka lima adhinta lahum*), *ʿabasta wa tawallā* (80:1), *tuḥarrim* ("you forbid", 66:1). Where *-ka* marks what is *the Prophet's* (possession, relation), *-ta* marks what the Prophet *did or should do* — and, strikingly, its most distinctive use is the register of **gentle reproach** (9:43; 80:1–10; 66:1). A raw count of *-ta* is even less usable than *-ka* as a corpus-wide signal, because every second-person perfect verb ends in it regardless of addressee, so it is heavily referent-blind and must be read with the referent filter [INFERRED-referent; flagged]. But the qualitative point is secure and sharpens the placement: **al-Kawthar contains no *-ta* at all** — no reproach, no commanded *qul* — only the *-ka* of gift and relation, plus two fresh imperatives (*ṣalli*, *nḥar*). On the joint *-ka* / *-ta* map it is therefore *pure gift-and-response, with neither office nor correction* [MEASURED for the absence; the register characterisation of *-ta* is INFERRED].

**The regulative voice: a gift that does not over-burden.** One facet of personal address deserves separate notice because it bears on verse 2. Across the address to the Prophet there is a recurring *regulative* movement in which God **eases or restrains** rather than loads: "We did not send down the Qurʾān to you that you be distressed" (*li-tashqā*, 20:2); the night-vigil of 73:1–4 is explicitly relaxed — "recite what is easy" (*mā tayassara*, 73:20); "We shall ease you toward ease" (*nuyassiruka lil-yusrā*, 87:8); "perhaps you would destroy yourself in grief over them" (*bākhiʿun nafsaka*, 18:6; 26:3); and the reproach of over-scruple, "why do you forbid what God has made lawful to you?" (66:1) [TEXT]. Underwritten generally by "God burdens no soul beyond its capacity" (2:286) and "God wishes to lighten for you" (4:28). The dominant pattern is *zeal or love that over-extends, lovingly calibrated back to capacity*; 66:1 is the clearest case of genuine self-imposed over-restriction met by divine easing [INFERRED, ~85%]. Against this backdrop, the command of 108:2 reads in a particular way: *fa-ṣalli li-rabbika wa-nḥar* is **minimal and unquantified** — two acts (turn to your Lord; give back), with no "all night", no "until", no regimen like 73:1–4 [MEASURED]. It asks for *completeness* (prayer plus sacrifice = a total return, §5.15) but in *bounded, capacity-scaled* form. The required response to the gift is real, but it is proportioned to the person and never crushing — a point we take up theologically in §6.6.

### 5.25.1 The *innā …-ka* family: al-Kawthar among the divine declarations to the Prophet

*Innā aʿṭaynāka* (108:1) is not a unique opening: it instantiates a recognisable corpus frame — *innā* ("Indeed We") + a first-person-plural verb + the Prophet in the second person. Enumerating every āyah that opens with *innā* and addresses the Prophet (excluding the *kadhālika* "thus" formulas and the Noah verse, which are not second-person to Muḥammad) yields twelve members [MEASURED]. They differentiate along two axes at once — *what* is declared, and *which* second-person form carries it (the *laka*/ـك distinction of §5.25):

*{{T7}}. The* innā …-ka *family — divine first-person-plural declarations to the Prophet, by theme and by second-person grammar.*

| Verse(s) | Declaration | Verb (We-…) | 2nd-person form | Category |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 108:1 | *gave **you** al-Kawthar* | *aʿṭaynā-**ka*** | object ـك ("you") | **GIFT** |
| 15:95 | *sufficed **you** against the mockers* | *kafaynā-**ka*** | object ـك ("you") | **DEFENCE** |
| 48:1 | *opened **for you** a clear victory* | *fataḥnā **laka*** | benefactive لك ("for you") | **VICTORY / GRANT** |
| 2:119; 35:24; 48:8 | *sent **you** — a bearer of tidings, a warner, a witness* | *arsalnā-**ka*** | object ـك ("you") | **MISSION** |
| 4:105; 39:2 | *sent down **to you** the Book* | *anzalnā ilay-**ka*** | directional إليك ("to you") | **REVELATION** |
| 39:41 | *sent down **upon you** the Book for mankind* | *anzalnā ʿalay-**ka*** | directional عليك ("upon you") | **REVELATION** |
| 4:163 | *inspired **you**, as the prophets before* | *awḥaynā ilay-**ka*** | directional إليك | **REVELATION** |
| 76:23 | *We — **We** — sent down **upon you** the Qurʾān* | *(naḥnu) nazzalnā ʿalay-**ka*** | directional عليك (+ emphatic *naḥnu*) | **REVELATION** |
| 73:5 | *shall cast **upon you** a weighty word* | *sa-nulqī ʿalay-**ka*** | directional عليك | **REVELATION** |

Three observations follow [MEASURED unless noted]. First, the second-person grammar **tracks the category**: revelation is *sent to / upon* the Prophet (directional *ilayka* / *ʿalayka* — the Book arrives at him); mission, defence, and gift make him the **direct object** (*arsalnāka*, *kafaynāka*, *aʿṭaynāka* — "We Xed *you*"); and victory alone takes the **benefactive *laka*** (*fataḥnā laka*, "opened *for* you", 48:1 — the very form you isolate, and the family's only one). Second, *al-Kawthar* sits in the **object-ـك, gift** cell, and its nearest sibling is **15:95**, *innā kafaynāka l-mustahziʾīn* — "We have sufficed *you* against the mockers": same *innā*, same object-ـك, and the theme of **divine defence against revilers** that 108:3 enacts when it turns the *shāniʾ* into *al-abtar*. Where the corpus distributes the grant and the defence across two verses (48:1 the opening, 15:95 the sufficing), **al-Kawthar fuses gift and defence in a single sūra** (v1 gift, v3 vindication) [INFERRED, ~80%]. Third, the contrast with revelation is itself meaningful: the Book is *sent to* the Prophet to be conveyed onward (the office register of §5.25), whereas *al-Kawthar* is *given to him* as his own — reinforcing that this sūra is personal gift, not transmission. The *innā aʿṭaynāka* of 108, then, is one node in a small, well-defined lattice, and its coordinates — object-ـك, gift, fused with defence — are exactly what set it apart within that lattice.

### 5.25.2 Synthesis: the modes of address to the Prophet, enumerated and tallied

The forms surveyed above are not a loose list; they fall on **two crossing axes**, and tallying them across the corpus locates al-Kawthar precisely. The first axis is the **terminus**: does the address *rest on* the Prophet (its content is about or for him) or *pass through* him (he is the conduit to an audience)? The second is **register**: is he hailed by **office/title**, by **intimate epithet**, or addressed with **no summons** at all? {{T8}} enumerates the modes, gives a corpus tally, and marks each as present or absent in al-Kawthar.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transmissive imperative | *qul* ("say!") | **294** | through → people | office (personal *command*, public *content*) | **absent (0)** |
| Legislative query | *yasʾalūnaka … qul* ("they ask you… say") | 9 | through → people | office | absent |
| Vocative, by office | *yā ayyuhā l-nabī / l-rasūl* | 15 (13 + 2) | through (legislative) | office / title | absent |
| Vocative, by epithet | *yā ayyuhā l-muzzammil / l-muddaththir* | 2 | on him (mission-call) | intimate | absent |
| Revelation, directional | *anzalnā **ilayka** / **ʿalayka*** | 77† / 57† | through (the Book, to convey) | office-leaning | absent |

**Differentiate.** The single most frequent way the Qurʾān addresses the Prophet is *qul* — 294 times — and *qul* is the proof that address can be **personal in form yet other-directed in purpose**: grammatically a command to him, functionally a script for the people. Around it cluster the other *through-him* modes (the legislative *yā ayyuhā l-nabī*, the directional *anzalnā ilayka* by which revelation arrives to be conveyed). By contrast the *on-him* modes — the declarative gift-family (12), the intimate epithets (2), the possessives, the self-act imperatives — are comparatively rare and mark the moments when the address *rests on the Prophet himself*.

**Relate and synthesize.** Read against this tally, al-Kawthar's profile is unambiguous: **it uses only *on-him*, personal modes and none of the *through-him*, office modes.** No *qul* (against 294 in the corpus), no office-vocative (against 15), no directional-revelation form, no *-ta* reproach — but a declarative gift in the object *-ka* (*aʿṭaynāka*), two possessives (*rabbika*, *shāniʾaka*), and two self-act imperatives the Prophet *performs* (*ṣalli, nḥar*). The "both aspects" that *qul* holds together — personal command, public content — are in al-Kawthar resolved entirely toward the personal: its commands are the Prophet's *own* worship, not speech relayed onward; even the surah's *daʿwa* affinity (§5.20.3), which *would* be transmissive, is not what its imperatives enjoin. The gift received, the Lord turned to, the hater turned back, and the worship commanded form **a closed loop that begins and ends on the Prophet**. This is the quantified form of the headline of §5.25: among all the ways the Qurʾān speaks to its Prophet, al-Kawthar is the purest instance of speaking *to* him rather than *through* him — the most self-terminating, least transmissive address in the book [MEASURED for every tally and for the present/absent profile; INFERRED, ~85%, for the two-axis synthesis].

Underlying all of this is the self-interpreting web itself: {{F12}} renders that web in *directed* form for the sūra's own terms — each hapax fixed by its common context (P=1.0), not the reverse — the very mechanism by which a rare word like *kawthar* can be read at all (cf. §5.23, §6.7).
