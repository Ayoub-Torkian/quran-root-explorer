## 5.10 A closer look: why "We gave" (*innā*) and not "I gave" (*innī*)?

The surah opens *innā aʿṭaynāka* — "**We** have given you." A natural question is why the divine speaker here is the first-person **plural** (*innā* / "We"), when elsewhere God speaks in the first-person **singular** (*innī* / *innanī* / "I"). Arabic, like several languages, has a "plural of majesty" — the royal "We" — so the alternation is not about number but about **register**. The Qur'an's own usage, counted across the corpus, shows the rule clearly, and the surah itself observes it in miniature.

First, the raw distribution. The emphatic opener *innā* ("indeed We") occurs **208 times** in the text, the singular *innī* **151 times** and its lengthened twin *innanī* **7 times**; the bare pronouns pattern the same way (*naḥnu*, "We", 86×) [MEASURED]. So neither form is rare or marked as unusual; each is the *default* for a different kind of statement.

Second — and this is the substance — the two forms *tend* to sort by **theme** (Figure 10). The plural "We" is the regular register of God's proclamatory acts of **power and bounty**: creating, sending down revelation, and granting. The proclamatory grants and gifts addressed to the Prophet that parallel 108:1 are cast in this plural, in the same shape: "Indeed **We** have opened for **you** a clear victory" (48:1, *innā fataḥnā laka*); "**We** gave **you** the seven oft-repeated [verses]" (15:87, *ātaynāka*); "Did **We** not expand for **you** your breast?" (94:1, *nashraḥ laka*); "**We** sent down to **you** the Qur'an" (76:23) [TEXT]. The same plural carries the great acts of power — "**We** sent it down on the Night of Ordainment" (97:1); "**We** created everything in measure" (54:49) [TEXT]. One narrower fact is exact rather than merely typical: the Qur'an **never** casts divine *giving* in the first-person singular — there is no *innī aʿṭaytu* or *innī ātaytu* anywhere in the text; the verbs of bestowal (*aʿṭā*, *ātā*) appear in the divine first person only in the plural [MEASURED]. The gift of *kawthar* takes that grammar of largesse.

The singular "I", by contrast, clusters around a different theme: **oneness, nearness, and the demand to be worshipped.** "Indeed **I** am Allah; there is no god but **I**, so worship **Me**" (20:14); "there is no god but **I**, so worship **Me**" (21:25); "Indeed **I** am near" (2:186); "He is One God, so be in awe of **Me** alone" (16:51) [TEXT]. The cleanest piece of evidence is a **minimal pair** on the same verb, "to create." In the majesty register God says *khalaqnā*, "**We** created" (54:49; 76:2); but at 51:56, where creation is tied directly to the purpose of worship, the verb switches to the singular: "**I** did not create (*khalaqtu*) jinn and humans except that they worship **Me**" [TEXT]. Same act, two registers — plural when the frame is power, singular when the frame is the worship of the one God.

Third, the surah **toggles between the two registers in adjacent verses**, which is the decisive internal confirmation. Verse 1 bestows the gift in the plural of majesty ("**We** have given"); but verse 2, turning to worship, does **not** say "pray to **Us**." It says "pray to **your Lord**" — *li-rabbika*, the singular Lord — exactly the register the corpus reserves for worship and oneness [TEXT]. Had verse 1 read *innī aʿṭaytuka* ("**I** have given you"), it would have done two unwanted things at once: dropped the majesty proper to a royal grant, and pre-empted the singular, worship-directed focus that verse 2 places on "your Lord." The chosen wording keeps each register in its place — "We" for the bounty, "your Lord" for the worship — and so the very alternation the question asks about turns out to be part of the surah's design, not an accident of style.

A related question sharpens the same point: why not the **doubly emphatic** form, *innā naḥnu aʿṭaynā* — "Indeed **We, it is We**, have given" — on the model of 15:9, *innā naḥnu nazzalnā l-dhikr* ("Indeed it is **We** who sent down the Reminder")? Here too the corpus shows what the extra word does. Adding the separate pronoun *naḥnu* ("We") on top of the already-plural verb is not idle doubling; it is a pronoun of **emphasis and restriction** — "it is We *and no other*." And it surfaces, in divine speech, precisely where God's agency is being asserted **against a rival or a denial**: "We sent down the Reminder, and We *will guard it*" (15:9, against corruption); "it is **We** who give life and bring death, and **We** are the inheritors" (15:23; 50:43, against other claimed powers); "We sent down to you the Qur'an" (76:23, against doubters of its source) [TEXT]. The clearest case is the great contrast of Sūrat al-Wāqiʿa, where *naḥnu* is set point-blank against *antum* ("you"): "Is it **you** who create it, or are **We** the creator? … Is it **you** who bring it down from the clouds, or is it **We**?" (56:59, 56:69) [TEXT]. The doubled pronoun, in short, is the grammar of **polemic** — exclusive divine agency pressed against someone who would deny or rival it. Sūrat al-Kawthar has no such rival to refute about *who gave the gift*; it is a direct, consoling **announcement of bounty** to the Prophet. So the unmarked *innā aʿṭaynāka* is exactly fitted to it: the emphasis (*inna*) falls on the **certainty of the gift**, not on excluding an alternative giver. The surah's one contrastive stroke is held back for verse 3 — and even there it concerns the *enemy's fate*, not a dispute over the Giver. We state the confidence carefully, including against the surah's grain. **[MEASURED] and exact:** the form counts (*innā* 208, *innī* 151, *innanī* 7); the absence of any singular divine "I gave" (*innī*/*innanī* with *aʿṭā*/*ātā* = zero occurrences); the plural parallels (48:1; 15:87; 94:1; 76:23); and the *khalaqnā* / *khalaqtu* pair. **[INFERRED] and a tendency, not a law:** that the alternation is *governed* by a "majesty/bounty versus oneness/worship" register. This must be qualified, because clear **counter-cases** exist — the recurring frame "when your Lord said to the angels, '*Indeed I* (*innī*) am placing / about to create…'" (2:30; 15:28; 38:71) uses the singular for an act of making, and "*Indeed I* have chosen **you**, O Moses" (7:144) is a singular grant to a prophet. The pattern is therefore best read as: the singular marks **intimate or relational** speech (oneness, nearness, the call to worship Me, and direct dialogue with angels or a prophet), while the proclamatory **plural** marks majestic bounty — with the bestowal-verbs *aʿṭā*/*ātā* falling, as it happens, entirely on the plural side. What would sharpen this further is a full thematic tagging of all 366 cases. On the present evidence, the best-supported answer to "why *innā* and not *innī*" is: 108:1 is a proclamatory bestowal of bounty, the register the Qur'an regularly (and, for the giving-verbs, invariably) renders with the royal "We," after which verse 2 deliberately shifts to the singular "your Lord" for the act of worship.
