## 5.11 The verb of the gift: why *aʿṭā*, and not *ātā*, *wahaba*, *iṣṭafā* or *ijtabā*?

Verse 1 says God *aʿṭā* ("gave") the Prophet *al-Kawthar*. Arabic has a whole family of verbs for granting, gifting, conferring, and electing, and the Qur'an uses them with remarkable consistency — each keeps its own lane. Reading the **whole dataset** of each verb (the method again: a word is known by all its uses) shows why *aʿṭā* is the right one here, and why three obvious alternatives are not. Table 4 summarizes; the prose explains.

| Verb (root) | Times | What it characteristically gives (read from all its uses) | If used in 108:1 it would imply |
|---|--:|---|---|
| *aʿṭā* (ʿ–ṭ–w) | 14 | a **free, unbounded gift**; often the bare noun "a gift" (*ʿaṭāʾ*), and a gift that **satisfies** (93:5) or is "**not cut off**" (11:108) | a pure, open-ended bounty — *chosen here* |
| *ātā* (ʾ–t–y) | 549 | a **named endowment**: the Book, wisdom, judgement, kingdom, knowledge (2:269; 2:251; 3:79; 15:87) | a specific, defined grant (like "the Book") — too narrow |
| *wahaba* (w–h–b) | 25 | a gift of **offspring / heirs** and mercy (Isaac, Ishmael, John, Solomon; 19:5, 19:19, 21:90, 38:30) | "We grant you **progeny**" — fighting the taunt on its own terms |
| *iṣṭafā* (ṣ–f–w) | 18 | **election of a person** for a role (Adam, Noah, Abraham's line, Mary, Moses; 3:33, 3:42, 7:144) | "We **chose** you" — an office, not a bounty |
| *ijtabā* (j–b–y) | 12 | **election / picking-out** of a prophet (Joseph 12:6; Adam 20:122; the Prophet 22:78) | "We **selected** you" — again election, not gift |

Start with *aʿṭā* itself. Across its fourteen occurrences it is the verb of **free, unconditioned bestowal**, and three features make it ideal for *kawthar*. First, its object is characteristically **open-ended** — sometimes simply the noun "a gift" (38:39, "this is Our *ʿaṭāʾ*, so withhold or bestow"; 78:36, "a reward, a *gift* (*ʿaṭāʾ*) from your Lord"; 17:20, "We extend the *bounty* of your Lord"). Second, it is tied to the recipient's **satisfaction and sufficiency** — and here the corpus hands us a near-twin of our verse: at 93:5, "*your Lord will give you* (*yuʿṭīka*) *and you will be pleased*" — same verb, same Lord-to-Prophet direction, same note of a giving that fills and contents [TEXT]. Third, and most beautifully, *aʿṭā*'s gift can be one that **does not end**: at 11:108 the people of Paradise receive "*a gift* (*ʿaṭāʾ*) *never cut off*" — *ghayr majdhūdh*, using a verb of severance [TEXT]. A gift that is "not cut off" is the precise antithesis of *al-abtar*, "the cut-off one," in verse 3. The verb of verse 1 already leans toward continuation; the surah's spine is in its grammar.

Now the alternatives, each excluded for a reason the corpus makes visible. *ātā* (the most common give-verb, 549×) is the verb of **named endowments** — it gives "the Book and wisdom," "kingdom and wisdom," "judgement and prophethood," "knowledge." The user's own example shows it exactly: "He gives (*yuʾtī*) **wisdom** to whom He wishes, and whoever is given wisdom is given **abundant good** (*khayran kathīran*)" (2:269) — where *ātā* takes a definite gift (wisdom) and "abundant good" is the *result* described [TEXT]. Tellingly, when the Qur'an gives the Prophet a *specific* endowment it does use *ātā*: "We have given you (*ātaynāka*) the seven oft-repeated [verses] and the great Qur'an" (15:87). Had 108:1 read *ātaynāka l-kawthar*, it would have tagged *kawthar* as one more defined endowment alongside the Book and wisdom — closing down the very open-endedness that makes "abundance" what it is.

*wahaba* (25×) is the decisive contrast, and it carries the surah's deepest point. Reading its whole dataset, *wahaba* is overwhelmingly the verb of the **gift of offspring** (and of mercy): "grant me a good offspring" (3:38), "grant me an heir" (19:5), "a pure son" (19:19), "We gave him Isaac and Jacob" (6:84; 19:49; 21:72; 29:27), "We gave him John and cured his wife's infertility" (21:90), "to David We gave Solomon" (38:30) [TEXT]. If the surah's purpose were to promise the Prophet **descendants** — to answer a sneer about a man with no surviving sons on its own terms — the expected verb was *wahaba*, the progeny-gift verb. The surah does **not** use it. Nor does it use any word for child, son, or lineage at all: its seven roots are giving, abundance, prayer, Lord, sacrifice, hatred, and severance — **no progeny vocabulary whatever** [MEASURED]. This silence is eloquent. The Qur'an declines to meet the taunt of "you are cut off (childless)" by promising children; it answers instead with *aʿṭā* + *kawthar* — an *unbounded abundance* — thereby relocating "continuation" away from biological lineage and onto the divine gift itself. The verb choice and the architecture say the same thing: legacy is redefined, not merely restored [INFERENCE].

Finally, *iṣṭafā* and *ijtabā* (18× and 12×) are not gift-verbs at all but **election-verbs**: they *choose a person* for a role — Adam, Noah, the line of Abraham, Mary (3:33, 3:42), Moses (7:144), Joseph (12:6), and indeed the Prophet himself elsewhere ("He has chosen you," 22:78). They answer the question "chosen for what?" rather than "given what?" The Qur'an plainly *has* these verbs available for the Prophet; it does not use them in 108:1 because the message of the surah is not the conferral of an office but the bestowal of a bounty — for which *aʿṭā* is exactly the word.

In sum: *aʿṭā* is chosen because *al-Kawthar* is a **free, open, continuing gift that contents its receiver** — not a named endowment (*ātā*), not a grant of progeny (*wahaba*), not an election to office (*iṣṭafā* / *ijtabā*). And the avoidance of *wahaba* in particular is itself an argument: the surah will not reduce the gift to descendants.
