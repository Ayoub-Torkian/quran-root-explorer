## 5.16 Lit by its opposite; and the gift that is not withdrawn

The self-interpreting method works not only through synonyms and semantic fields but through **antonyms**: a word is illuminated by what the Qur'an sets against it. Two opposites sharpen this surah.

**Much against little (*kathīr* vs *qalīl*).** The natural opposite of *kawthar*'s "muchness" is *qalīl*, "fewness," which the Qur'an uses 76 times — and the pairing carries a lesson that bears directly on what *kawthar* is and is not. Again and again, raw quantity is *demoted*: "how **many** a **small** band (*fi'a qalīla*) has overcome a **large** band (*fi'a kathīra*) by God's leave!" (2:249); "if there be twenty steadfast among you, they will overcome two hundred" (8:65) [TEXT]. Numerousness as such does not decide; God's aid does. This is the same demotion of mere quantity we saw inside the *k-th-r* field itself (the cautionary "most of them…", §5.2). Setting *kawthar* against *qalīl* therefore makes a sharp point: the gift of verse 1 is **not** praised as "a large amount." Were sheer bigness the value, the Qur'an's own usage would already have undercut it. *Kawthar* is the **good, God-given** abundance — abundance qualified by its source and its use — not quantity for its own sake.

**Giving against withholding (*aʿṭā* vs *amsaka*) — and the irrevocable gift.** The opposite of giving is *amsaka*, "to withhold, hold back" (27×), and here the Qur'an makes a claim that secures *kawthar* from a new direction. "**Whatever mercy God unfolds for people, none can withhold it** (*fa-lā mumsika lahā*); and whatever He withholds, none can release after Him" (35:2) [TEXT]. What God opens, no power can shut; what God gives is not subject to seizure or cancellation by any external hand. Set beside the human disposition — "even if you possessed the treasuries of mercy, you would *withhold* them, fearing to spend" (17:100) — the contrast is stark: the human fist closes, the divine hand stays open, and what it opens stays open. This adds to *kawthar*'s continuity (§5.14) a guarantee of **security**: the gift not only lasts in time, it cannot be wrested away. (God's freedom to expand or straiten provision as He wills — *yabsuṭ* / *yaqdir*, 2:245; 13:26; 17:30 — is the same sovereignty stated on the other side: the open hand is open by will, not by obligation.)

**The single condition: forfeit only by self-reversal.** If the divine gift is irrevocable, can it ever be lost? The Qur'an's answer is exact and returns us to the principle of §5.13. "God never changes a **blessing** (*niʿma*) He has bestowed on a people **until they change what is in themselves**" (8:53); "God does not change a people's condition **until they change what is in themselves**" (13:11) [TEXT]. The gift is permanent from the Giver's side; the only thing that can turn it to its opposite is the **receiver turning himself** to the opposite — gratitude curdling into ingratitude, reception into the grasping of *takāthur*. The favour is not arbitrarily withdrawn; it is forfeited, and only from within. This is the precise mechanism behind the "two soils" of §5.13: the rain keeps falling (the gift is not withheld), but the ground can turn itself to stone.

Folded back into the surah, these opposites complete the picture with unusual finality. *Kawthar* is given (§5.11), lasting (§5.14), and — by 35:2 — **unwithholdable**: no enemy's sneer can cancel it. It can be lost only if its holder reverses himself, which the Prophet, commanded to prayer and sacrifice in verse 2, precisely does not. The *hater* of verse 3, by contrast, is *abtar* not because a gift was seized from him by force but because his own enmity is the self-reversal that cuts him off; he is, in the language of 8:53 and 13:11, the one who "changed what was in himself." So the surah's two outcomes are not a lottery of divine favour and disfavour but the lawful result of two stances toward one open, unwithholdable, abundant hand [INFERENCE].

We mark the confidence. **[MEASURED]:** *qalīl* (76×) and the "small band overcomes the large" verses (2:249; 8:65); *amsaka* (27×) with 35:2's *fa-lā mumsika lahā* and 17:100; the expand/straiten verses (2:245; 13:26; 17:30); and 8:53 / 13:11 on change-only-by-self-change. **[INFERRED]:** that *kawthar* is thereby marked as *qualified* abundance rather than mere quantity; that the gift is *secure/irrevocable* (35:2 speaks specifically of *mercy*, which we take as the paradigm divine gift); and that the surah's two fates are self-incurred stances toward one open hand. These are readings, but each sits on an explicit verse, and together they close the loop opened in §5.13: the gift is given, lasting, and unwithholdable — lost only by the one who turns it, and himself, to the opposite.
