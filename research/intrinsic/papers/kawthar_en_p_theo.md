## 6.6 The gift, merit, and justice: grace, *saʿy*, and the demonstrable claim

The surah's opening — a gift announced, *innā aʿṭaynāka* — invites a serious objection, and it is better stated than suppressed. A gift given *for nothing* is, by definition, unearned; how then can it be a merit, and does singling one recipient out not look like favouritism, even injustice? "Why him, and not me?" The objection is old and not a straw man, and we take it at full strength.

Two observations frame the reply, both internal. First, **the Qurʾān itself voices the objection.** Of the deniers it reports: "We will not believe until we are given the like of what God's messengers were given" (*lan nuʾmina ḥattā nuʾtā mithla mā ūtiya rusulu-llāh*, 6:124) — which is precisely "why him, not me?" — and the answer stands in the same verse: "God knows best where to place His message" (*Allāhu aʿlamu ḥaythu yajʿalu risālatah*) [TEXT]. And the disposition behind the complaint is named: "Or do they envy people for the bounty (*faḍl*) God has given them?" (4:54) [TEXT]. Scripture poses the objection in its sharpest form and replies on the spot.

**The reply turns on a distinction the objection blurs: justice is not equality.** Justice (*ʿadl*) is rendering each his *desert*; equality (*musāwāh*) is rendering each the *same*. Injustice (*ẓulm*) is the denial of a *right* (*ḥaqq*) or the punishment of the undeserving — "your Lord wrongs no one" (18:49), "God wrongs not an atom's weight" (4:40) [TEXT]. A gift to A denies B nothing B was *owed*: B had no entitlement to A's gift, so B is not wronged. These verses guarantee that no one is denied his *desert*, not that all receive the *same grace*. And grace (*faḍl*) is precisely not a wage (*ajr*): a wage is earned and owed, grace is given beyond desert. Nor is its placement arbitrary — "God knows best where to place His message" (6:124) sets the gift by knowledge (the *ʿalīm* of *wāsiʿun ʿalīm*, §5.18), in a recipient fit to bear it [INFERRED, ~85%].

**Reconciling the gift with "man has only what he strove for" (53:39).** The deepest form of the objection invokes *laysa lil-insāni illā mā saʿā* — which appears, on its face, to forbid any unearned gift. The resolution is that the verse and the gift inhabit **two different economies**. Verse 53:39 governs the economy of *recompense* (*jazāʾ*): what one is judged and rewarded for in the hereafter — and there one reaps exactly one's own striving (it follows 53:38, "no bearer bears another's burden"). This is the domain of *desert*. *Faḍl*, by contrast, is the economy of *capital*, not harvest: existence, faculties, prophethood, *kawthar* — none is earned; each is the seed and soil one is given. But the *harvest* is by striving. Grace supplies the capital; *saʿy* determines the return. The surah shows the seam in its own structure: the pure grace of verse 1 is immediately **yoked to a commanded striving** in verse 2 (*fa-ṣalli wa-nḥar*) — the gift is not "for nothing" in the sense of bearing fruit for nothing; what it *yields* depends entirely on the response (the soil of §5.13; "if you give thanks I will surely increase you", 14:7). The reward-asymmetry of §5.12.1 fits exactly: reward is *multiplied* by grace (tenfold, sevenhundredfold, *yuḍāʿif*), punishment is *exact* desert (*lā yujzā illā mithlahā*, 6:160) — grace only ever raises the ceiling above one's striving, never lowers the floor below it. So 53:39 is the floor (you always receive at least your *saʿy*); *faḍl* is the ceiling (grace may give more). They do not compete.

**The gift as an instance of a universal law: divine defence-by-enabling.** A further internal principle removes the appearance of partiality. "Indeed God defends those who believe" (*inna-llāha yudāfiʿu ʿani-lladhīna āmanū*, 22:38) — and the next verses show *how* that defence operates: "permission is given (*udhina*) to those fought against, because they were wronged; and God is able to help them" (22:39), then "were it not for God's repelling (*dafʿ*) people by means of one another…" (22:40) [TEXT]. Divine defence works by *granting capacity / enabling*. The differential gift is therefore not favouritism toward one person but an *instance of a universal law*: God defends everyone in the category of faith (*alladhīna āmanū*), and one mode of that defence is to give capacity, in the form, measure, and timing His knowledge assigns. Sūrat al-Kawthar is itself a token of 22:38: verse 1 gives (the enabling), verse 3 turns back the reviler — gift *plus* vindication against the *shāniʾ* is exactly God defending His believer against the one who wrongs him [INFERRED, ~85%; the link is thematic, not single-verse].

**The required return is capacity-scaled, so the gift is never a crushing debt.** As shown in §5.25, the address to the Prophet is marked by a regulative voice that *eases* rather than loads (20:2; 73:20; 87:8; 2:286; 4:28). If even the Prophet's commanded response is proportioned to capacity and actively lightened, then the gift is never converted into an unpayable obligation — which would itself be a kind of injustice. The economy is humane on both ends: grace gives the capital, asks a *proportioned* return (*saʿy*, real but bounded), and trims the load to the soul. This strengthens the answer to "why him, not me?": the gift carries no burden its recipient cannot bear, and the striving demanded is sized to the bearer [INFERRED, ~80%].

**The demonstrable claim, and a warrant for the method.** One move must be refused: "God knows best" (6:124) is sufficient *for the one who already trusts*, but it is not, by itself, a *justification to the one who does not know* — and the Qurʾān itself refuses to leave the unknowing without justification. God sends messengers "so that mankind would have no argument (*ḥujjah*) against God after the messengers" (4:165); "We never punish until We send a messenger" (17:15); else they would plead, "Our Lord, why did You not send us a messenger…?" (20:134; 28:47); and "your Lord would not destroy towns for wrongdoing while their people were heedless" (6:131) [TEXT]. God's own practice thus sets a value: furnish the proof, remove the excuse, make the reasoning *answerable* to those who do not yet see it. By that same standard the resolution of the merit-objection must be *shown*, not asserted as mystery. Read at the level of method, 4:165's "no *ḥujjah* after the messengers" is a **demonstrative imperative**, and it is precisely what this study's method enacts: *al-Qurʾān yufassiru baʿḍuhu baʿḍā* plus exhaustive corpus coverage is the making of each reading *publicly demonstrable* against the text, rather than asserted from outside. The discipline of showing the work — nothing external admitted as evidence, every claim tagged and measured — is itself an enactment of the principle in 4:165 [INFERRED, ~80%].

**Honest residue.** What is answered here is the charge of *injustice*: no one is denied a right; the gift is a universal defence-law instantiated, not partiality; judgement falls on the *use* of what one is given, capacity-calibrated, not on the endowment; and the reasoning is made demonstrable rather than asserted. What is *not* fully closed is the deepest theodicy — *why* people are born with unequal capacity-to-believe-and-strive at all, the distribution of the enabling itself. The argument pushes that question one level back rather than dissolving it, and a fair critic may press there. We state this plainly. (The internal evidence we do have points away from condemnation of the unendowed: "We never punish until We send a messenger", 17:15; 6:131.) [INFERRED ~85%; what would flip it: a text condemning someone for lacking an enabling never given.] On 53:39 and the harmony of *saʿy* with grace, Ṭabāṭabāʾī (*al-Mīzān*) and Jawādī Āmulī (*Tasnīm*) argue at length that striving is the basis and grace builds upon it, the enabling to strive being itself a grace [REPORT — positions, to be verified against the texts before being relied on].

## 6.7 Why the referent is left open: *muḥkam*, *mutashābih*, *taʾwīl*, and the screen

A reader may finally ask why the central gift is *unspecified* — why the surah names *kawthar* without saying what it is. This is not a defect to be repaired but, the Qurʾān says of itself, a designed feature; and reflecting on it clarifies both the surah and the limits of our own instrument.

**Two registers, declared by the text.** "It is He who sent down to you the Book; in it are precise verses (*muḥkamāt*) — they are the foundation of the Book (*umm al-kitāb*) — and others multivalent (*mutashābihāt*)" (3:7) [TEXT]. The Book itself states that it operates in two registers: some language fixed and univocal, some deliberately open. *Kawthar* belongs to the open register. Notably, 3:7 *enumerates neither* set — the boundary itself is left open, a meta-instance of the very openness it names; the exegetical tradition never produced an agreed membership [REPORT]. There is also a lexical reason particular to this word: *kawthar* is an intensive of the root *k-th-r* — it names *abundance-as-a-quality*, a **genus**, not a particular object [MEASURED/lexical]. A word meaning "God-given super-abundance" is built to be general; the many traditional readings (a river in paradise, *al-khayr al-kathīr*, the Qurʾān, prophethood, an enduring progeny) are not rival guesses to be narrowed to one winner but **instances of the one genus**. The method's result in §5.11 — meaning fixed, referent open — is the direct corollary.

**Bounded emergence, not relativism.** Does this mean the meaning is emergent, varying across persons and ages — and is that intended? Yes, but in a precise sense, between two errors. It is *not* relativism ("*kawthar* means whatever a reader wishes"): 3:7 condemns pursuing the open verses to manufacture *fitnah*, the *muḥkam* anchors meaning, and the antonymic *takāthur* (hoarded abundance) is explicitly *excluded* (§5.2). It is *not* single-fixed univocity either: the word is a genus and the text withheld the species. The intended middle is **bounded emergence**: the *meaning* is fixed (the genus — blessed, God-given, gratefully-received, fruitful abundance), while the *referent* is open and generative, realised differently for different people and times, but always within the fixed type — like a variable bound by a type. A river, prophethood, a faithful line, abundant good all satisfy the type; hoarded gold does not [INFERRED, ~85%]. The design rationale is twofold: *universality* (a genus lets the same ten words address every recipient of blessing in every age, where a fixed referent would bind them to one), and *participation* (an open referent makes the reader recognise the genus and locate his own instance — which is why the surah closes as a question of self-location). The same discipline covers the Qurʾān's other open language — the *muqaṭṭaʿāt* (الم), *al-ṣamad* (112:2), the great similitudes (24:35, "light upon light") — but *not* the *muḥkamāt* (inheritance shares, prohibitions, ritual forms), which are specified precisely because there univocity is the point. The Book uses both registers deliberately.

**Openness as a screen.** The openness is at once a blessing and a hazard, and the Qurʾān frames this duality as a *sorting* function. The same open verses, 3:7 says, are pursued by "those in whose hearts is deviation" for *fitnah*, while "those firmly grounded in knowledge say: we believe in it." The verse does not change; it manifests the reader. This is stated outright, and twice in nearly identical words: the gnat-parable and the number of the wardens are "only a trial (*fitnah*) / a parable (*mathal*)" by which God "misleads many and guides many" — believers gain certainty while "those in whose hearts is disease" say "what did God intend by this as a parable?" (*mādhā arāda-llāhu bi-hādhā mathalan*, 2:26; 74:31) [TEXT]. And "We send down of the Qurʾān that which is healing and mercy for the believers, but it increases the wrongdoers only in loss" (17:82; cf. 41:44) [TEXT]. The open device is a sieve: an identical stimulus, opposite outcomes, sorted by the state of the heart. This unifies the surah with a Qurʾān-wide design [INFERRED, ~80%]: the open *referent* of *kawthar* is the same structure as the open *verse* of 3:7 and the *test* of 2:26 — the gift becomes *kawthar* in the grateful heart and reveals the *abtar* in the resentful one (the *shāniʾ* of 108:3 is the "diseased heart" of 2:26, doing to the gift what that heart does to the open verse). The duality of blessing and pitfall is not a flaw in the text but the screening mechanism itself: it externalises the internal.

**Taʾwīl bounds the openness against relativism.** What keeps the open register from collapsing into "anything goes" is *taʾwīl*. The word is from أ-و-ل, *to return to the origin*; *taʾwīl* is the **return of the text to the reality it points to** — not a reading among many but a *real terminus*. The usage is consistent: Joseph says of the fulfilled dream, "this is the *taʾwīl* of my vision; my Lord has made it real (*ḥaqqā*)" (12:100); Khidr explains the *taʾwīl* of his puzzling acts as their hidden real reasons, adding "I did it not of my own accord" (18:82); and the disbelievers "await only its *taʾwīl* — the Day its *taʾwīl* comes" (7:53; cf. 10:39), where *taʾwīl* is the reality that *arrives* [TEXT]. Across the corpus *taʾwīl* is the objective reality the text returns to, often unfolding in time and "made *ḥaqq*" by God — so an open verse is not a blank screen but a *pointer with a destination*. Readings are therefore bounded: many understandings, one real *taʾwīl* they answer to. On who knows it, 3:7 admits two readings of the pause — "none knows its *taʾwīl* except God" (then: the grounded "say, we believe"), or "except God *and* those grounded in knowledge" — and *both exclude the free-for-all*: the *taʾwīl* belongs to God, or to God and the divinely-grounded, never to any reader [TEXT for the ambiguity; the two readings [REPORT]]. The paradigms confirm the qualified knower internally: Joseph is "chosen" and "taught the *taʾwīl* of events" (12:6), Khidr acts from "knowledge from Us" (18:65). And the screen sharpens here: the deviant "pursue its *taʾwīl*" (*ibtighāʾa taʾwīlih*, 3:7) — seizing at a reality they are unqualified to reach — while the grounded either know it by divine teaching or return it to God in trust.

**A *taʾwīl*-humble instrument.** This fixes the precise limit of the computational method, and turns the §5.11 reticence from a concession into a principle. Computation can *measure a gradient of openness* per verse — referent-resolvability, self-interpretation closure, register markers, polysemy load — and so *rank and localise* the open against the fixed, which is its demonstrated role throughout this program. It *cannot* claim that gradient **is** the *muḥkam* / *mutashābih* partition: 3:7 gives no criteria and the tradition no agreed list, so any computed binary is a **[HUMAN CONSTRUCT]** proxy with no ground truth to validate it, and on the relational reading of *tashābuh* a hard partition may be a category error. Most of all, the instrument must not pretend to *seize the taʾwīl* — to output "*the* referent of *kawthar* is X." That belongs to God, or to the divinely-grounded, and an algorithm announcing it would be doing exactly what 3:7 condemns. The method's refusal to manufacture a single referent is therefore not agnostic hand-waving but **fidelity to where the *taʾwīl* resides**: it returns the open word toward its reality far enough to bound the meaning, ranks the candidate referents for the human reader, and stops where seizure would begin. The arch is complete: the *muḥkam* anchors, the *mutashābih* opens, *taʾwīl* is the real terminus that bounds the openness, the screen sorts readers by how they approach that terminus, and the instrument localises without seizing. [INFERRED ~85%; the framework is [TEXT]-anchored (3:7; 12:100; 18:82; 7:53), the application to the method is interpretive.]
