## 5.15 The grammar of response: gift → "so" → worship

Verse 2 — *fa-ṣalli li-rabbika wa-nḥar*, "so pray to your Lord and sacrifice" — is not an arbitrary instruction tacked onto the gift. It is the second half of a recurring **grammar** the Qur'an uses whenever it records a divine favour: *favour* → *fa-* ("so") → *commanded response of grateful worship*. Reading the favour-verbs across the corpus shows both that this grammar is general and that al-Kawthar fills it with its own, uniquely total terms.

**Is the coupling unique to here?** For the verb *aʿṭā*, yes. Of its fourteen occurrences, **108 is the only one in which the giving is followed by a commanded act of worship** [MEASURED]. The others are bare statements ("your Lord will give you," 93:5), generalities ("withhold or bestow," 38:39), or descriptions of people (9:58). So the specific welding of *aʿṭā* to "so pray and sacrifice" belongs to this surah alone. But the *pattern* it instantiates is everywhere, carried by the other favour-words:

- **Election → prayer and almsgiving.** "He has *chosen* you (*ijtabākum*)… so *bow down, prostrate, worship your Lord*… *establish the prayer and give the alms*" (22:77–78) [TEXT]. The response to being elected is prayer (*ṣalāt*) plus a giving-back (*zakāt*).
- **Provision and security → "so let them worship."** The Quraysh surah: He "*fed them against hunger and secured them from fear*" — therefore "*let them worship the Lord of this House*" (106:3–4) [TEXT]. A gift of sustenance answered by worship, joined by the same logic of consequence.
- **Giving → generosity and proclamation.** The sister giving-surah, where "your Lord *will give* you (*yuʿṭīka*) and you will be pleased" (93:5), draws out the response with a run of *fa-* commands: "*so* as for the orphan, do not oppress him; as for the beggar, do not chide him; and as for the blessing of your Lord, *proclaim it*" (93:9–11) [TEXT]. Received bounty obliges generosity to others and public thanks.
- **The progeny-gift → praise.** "All praise belongs to God, who *gave me* (*wahaba lī*) Ishmael and Isaac" (14:39): *wahaba* answered by *ḥamd* [TEXT].
- **The Book → recitation and faith.** "Those to whom We *gave* (*ātaynā*) the Book *recite it as it ought to be recited and believe in it*" (2:121); the gift of revelation calls for following it, not concealing it [TEXT].

The common shape is unmistakable: **every divine favour summons a response of gratitude — worship turned to the Giver, giving turned to others, and acknowledgement spoken aloud** — and the connective is repeatedly the *fa-* of consequence (106; 93; 108). Al-Kawthar is one instance of this law, and the most concentrated.

**Why *ṣalāt*, and why *naḥr*?** Because together they are the *complete* grateful return, scaled to a complete gift. *Ṣalāt* is the self turned wholly toward the Giver — the recurring worship-term of the whole pattern (it is also the response in 22:78). *Naḥr* is the **giving-back of the gift**: from an abundance received, an offering returned, and returned "**to your Lord**" (*li-rabbika*) — God-ward, exactly as 6:162 frames it: "*my prayer and my sacrifice* (*nusuk*), *my living and my dying, are for God*" — prayer and sacrifice named together as the formula of total dedication [TEXT]. The pairing is deliberate. Where election is answered by prayer **+ alms** (*zakāt*, a measured portion, 22:78), the gift of sheer *abundance* is answered by prayer **+ sacrifice** (*naḥr*, the whole festal offering): a total gift met by a total return. A lesser response — words alone, or a metered due — would not match the measure of *kawthar*. The necessity is one of proportion: abundance given, abundance returned.

This also illuminates **why *naḥr* and *abtar* are each used only once** in the whole Qur'an (§5.1, §5.9). They are the surah's two **peaks** — the most concentrated form of the right response and of the wrong fate. *Naḥr* is the *maximal offering* (not the routine alms but the whole sacrifice); *abtar* is *maximal severance* (not mere lessening but the line wholly cut). Around them stand the common words — prayer, the ordinary vocabulary of cutting and perishing — while the two extremes are named with words the book uses nowhere else. The hapax mark the limits: total self-giving on one side, total being-cut-off on the other [INFERENCE].

So verse 2's necessity is now legible from the corpus. The gift of verse 1 *requires* a response by the Qur'an's own grammar (favour → *fa-* → worship); the response is *prayer and sacrifice* because that pair is the complete turning-to-and-returning-to the Giver; and the surah reaches for the maximal term, *naḥr*, because the gift it answers is maximal. The one who makes that return stands in verse 2; the one who refuses it — the *hater* — falls to the opposite peak in verse 3.

We mark the confidence. **[MEASURED]:** that 108 is the sole *aʿṭā* coupled with a worship-command; the favour-→-response pairs at 22:77–78, 106:3–4, 93:9–11, 14:39, 2:121; and 6:162's "prayer and sacrifice… for God." **[INFERRED]:** the unifying "favour → *fa-* → grateful worship/giving" grammar (well-supported by the *fa-* verses); the reading of *ṣalāt* + *naḥr* as a *total* return proportioned to a *total* gift; and the hapax-as-peak interpretation (the softest of these, an aesthetic-structural reading rather than a measured fact). The core point stands firmly: verse 2 is not an addendum but the required second beat of a pattern the Qur'an uses throughout — gift, then grateful worship.

### 5.15.1 The link itself: gift → *shukr* → increase (and the same law for everyone)

If the antithesis of verse 1 and verse 3 is plain, the bond of verse 1 to verse 2 needs spelling out — and the surah supplies the connective: the ***fa-*** ("so/therefore") that opens *fa-ṣalli*, the **fā' of consequence**. Grammatically, verse 2 is the *result* of verse 1; conceptually, that result is **gratitude (*shukr*)**: a received gift obligates a grateful response, and the Qur'an's fullest gratitude is worship returned to the Giver. The pattern is corpus-wide (106:3–4; 22:77–78; 93:5–11; 2:152), and *shukr* is enacted, not spoken: "**work** (*iʿmalū*), O family of David, **in gratitude**" (34:13). So prayer-and-sacrifice is gratitude *as deed*.

What makes the bond a living cycle rather than a settled debt is **14:7**: "*if you give thanks, I will surely increase you* (*la-azīdannakum*); and if you are ungrateful, My punishment is severe." Two features matter. First, the return on gratitude is not a fresh delivery of goods but an **enlargement of the self** — the object is *you* (*-kum*), capacity-growth (cf. §5.18). Second, the verse is addressed in the plural to **everyone**, so the law is not the Prophet's alone. The shape, then, is: **gift → grateful worship → increase of the receiver → greater capacity to receive and give.** Verse 2 is the hinge of that cycle.

**For the ordinary believer — read critically.** The same law holds universally — "were you to count God's favour you could not number it" (14:34; 16:18) — so every person stands where the Prophet stands in verse 1: a recipient who owes *shukr*. But the Qur'an adds a sober qualification for the general case that 108 (an ideal recipient, an irrevocable gift, 35:2) does not foreground: a gift is also a **test**. "Both the granting of ease and the tightening of provision are a trial" (89:15–16); wealth and children lavished on the heedless are "no good for them" but a **snare** (*istidrāj*; 3:178; 23:55–56). So for normal people the gift **reveals the soil** (§5.13): the grateful turn it into growth (the *kawthar* path); the ungrateful, into ruin (the *takāthur* / *abtar* path). The prescribed reaction is therefore clear — *shukr* expressed as worship and right use, never *kufr* (ingratitude) or competitive amassing — but, unlike the Prophet's case, the *outcome* is genuinely contingent on the response. That contingency is the examination. [TEXT: the *fa-*; 106:3–4; 22:77–78; 93:5–11; 2:152; 34:13; 14:7; 14:34; 89:15–16; 3:178. INFERRED: that the *fa-* encodes the *shukr* relation, and the extension of the cycle to the ordinary believer.]

**Worship as *operationalizing* the gift — and the knowledge-cycle.** There is a further dimension to *ṣalli li-rabbika* worth drawing out. To "pray to your Lord" is not only to thank in word; it is to **put the gift to work** — to turn received favour and guidance into enacted devotion and obedience. The Qur'an attaches its own growth-law to exactly this: "those who **strive in Us**, We shall surely **guide** them" (29:69); "those who are **guided, He increases them in guidance** and grants them their piety" (47:17); and the prayer it teaches is "*my Lord, **increase me in knowledge***" (20:114). So acting on what one has been given is itself the condition for being given more — guidance, capacity, knowledge — the same cycle 14:7 states for gratitude. (A well-known maxim — "whoever acts on what he knows, God bequeaths him knowledge of what he did not know" — captures the same idea, but as an external transmission we note it only as a **[REPORT]** resonance; the cycle's warrant here is the Qur'anic 29:69 / 47:17 / 20:114 / 14:7, not the maxim.) Verse 2, on this reading, is the hinge where the gift is *operationalized* and the cycle of increase begins. [TEXT: 29:69; 47:17; 20:114; 14:7. INFERRED: that *ṣalāt* here means operationalizing the gift, feeding the increase-cycle.]

### 5.15.2 When the good is for all, the call is still to one: al-Naṣr (110)

The closest structural parallel to al-Kawthar's gift-then-command is Sūrat al-Naṣr, and the comparison is most instructive exactly where the two differ. Al-Naṣr runs: "When the help of God and the victory come, and *you see* (*raʾayta*) the people entering God's religion in throngs — *then glorify the praise of your Lord* (*fa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika*) *and seek His forgiveness* (*wa-staghfirhu*); indeed He is ever-Relenting" (110:1–3) [TEXT]. The shape is al-Kawthar's exactly: a divine good, the consequential *fa-*, then a paired second-person command of worship turned to "*your Lord*" (*rabbika*). (Note that 110:2's *raʾayta*, "you saw," is one of the *-ta* verb-address forms catalogued in §5.25.)

But the *scope of the trigger* differs, and this is the point you isolate. Al-Kawthar's good is **personal and addressed** — *innā aʿṭaynāka*, "We gave *you*" (object *-ka*, §5.25.1): the gift is the Prophet's own. Al-Naṣr's good is **general and communal** — the help comes, the people convert in throngs; *nothing in the trigger is second-person* (the victory is for the whole community, "for everybody"). Yet the response is still routed through a personal command to the Prophet (*sabbiḥ*, *istaghfir*, *rabbika*). This is a measured observation about the grammar [MEASURED]: the favour→*fa-*→worship law (§5.15) holds whether the favour is private (a gift to the Prophet) or public (a victory for the umma) — in both, the prescribed response is the Prophet's *own* act of worship, voiced in the second person.

The *content* of the response differs too, and instructively. Al-Kawthar answers a gift with *ṣalāt + naḥr* (turning to the Giver + costly giving). Al-Naṣr answers a victory with *tasbīḥ + ḥamd + istighfār* — glorification, praise, and the seeking of forgiveness. The *istighfār* at the very moment of triumph is striking [INFERRED, ~80%]: the response to success is not a boast but humility and a turning-back to God — the precise opposite of the *takāthur* posture (§5.2), in which abundance curdles into self-congratulation. Read together, the two short sūras teach one lesson from two sides: a divine good — whether a private gift or a public victory — is met not by self-magnification but by worship returned to the Lord. The *fa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika* command is itself a small corpus family (15:98; 20:130; 40:55; 50:39; 52:48; 110:3), usually coupled with patience (*fa-ṣbir*) under provocation — the same forbearance al-Kawthar enjoins toward the hater by leaving him to God (v3) [MEASURED for the family; INFERRED for the link].
