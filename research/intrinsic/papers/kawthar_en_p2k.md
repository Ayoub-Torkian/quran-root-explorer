## 5.19 Why "your hater" (*shāniʾaka*) — not "your enemy" or "your adversary"?

Verse 3 could have used any of several available words for an opponent. It chose *shāniʾ*. Reading the alternatives across the corpus shows the choice is exact, and that it does decisive work for the surah's logic.

The Qur'an's common word for an opponent is *ʿaduww*, "enemy" (106×) — and its dataset is **external hostility**: Satan ("your manifest *enemy*," 2:168; 35:6), the disbelievers and the war-foe (60:1; 8:60). *ʿaduww* names a **relation of opposition** out in the world. A second word, *khaṣm* (18×), is the **adversary in a dispute** — the two litigants who climb the wall to David (38:21–22), "two contending parties" (22:19), the human as "an open *disputant*" (36:77). *Khaṣm* names a **case**, a two-sided contention with claims to be argued [TEXT]. The surah uses neither. It uses *shāniʾ* (only 3× in the whole Qur'an), which names not a relation or a role but an **inner state**: **the one who hates, detests, loathes** [MEASURED].

That choice matters for three reasons, all of them internal.

First, **it diagnoses a cause, not just labels a party.** We saw (§5.16) that a divine gift turns to its opposite only when a person "changes **what is in himself**" (8:53; 13:11). *Shāniʾ* names precisely that inner thing — **hatred** — and so it identifies the very mechanism of the man's undoing. He is *abtar* not because he occupies the role of "enemy," but because of what fills him: aversion to the source of good. To call him *ʿaduww* would have named his stance toward the Prophet; to call him *shāniʾ* names the disease in his own breast that cuts him off. The verdict (cut off) follows from the diagnosis (hating).

Second, **it is the true opposite of verse 2.** The response commanded in verse 2 is the grateful, loving **turning toward** the Lord (*ṣalāt* as connection, §5.17; *shukr* as enacted gratitude, §5.18). The exact contrary of that is not "an enemy" — a faction — but **aversion**, the heart turned *away* in loathing. The surah's deep antithesis is therefore an **inner-stance** one: gratitude-and-love (verse 2) against hatred (verse 3). *Shāniʾ* keeps the contrast on the plane where the surah actually operates — the posture of the heart toward the Giver — rather than dropping it to the plane of worldly factions.

Third — and most pointedly — **the corpus shows that hatred corrupts the one who holds it**, which is the key to the reversal. The only other two occurrences of *shāniʾ* are a single, repeated warning: "let not the **hatred** (*shanaʾān*) of a people lead **you** to injustice" (5:8; 5:2) [TEXT]. There, hatred is a force that endangers and distorts **its own bearer**, threatening to drag him into wrong. Read 108:3 in that light and the reversal becomes self-explanatory: the *shāniʾ*, the man consumed by *shanaʾān*, is undone **by his own hatred** — it is the inner corruption that severs him. The taunt he throws ("you are cut off") rebounds because the hatred behind it is itself the cutting blade, turned on the one who wields it. Naming him by his hatred is what makes the boomerang of verse 3 intelligible from the text alone.

A direct cross-reference sharpens this further — exactly the kind the method demands. Of the root's three occurrences, the **form** in 108:3 is found **nowhere else**. In 5:2 and 5:8 the word is *shanaʾān* (شَنَآنُ) — the **abstract noun, "hatred,"** the *emotion* ("let not the *hatred* of a people lead you to injustice"). Only in 108:3 does the root appear as *shāniʾ* (شَانِئَ) — the **active participle, "the hater,"** the *person* who embodies the emotion — and only here is it made personal with the possessive: *shāniʾa-**ka***, "**your** hater" [MEASURED]. The Qur'an thus names the *feeling* of hatred elsewhere and names the *person* who is hatred only here. Read together, the two forms trace a trajectory: hatred is, in 5:2/5:8, an emotion the believer is warned to **guard against** because it can corrupt him; 108:3 shows the end-state of one who did not guard against it but was wholly possessed by it, until he is no longer someone who *feels* hatred but someone who *is* "the hater" — and that person is *abtar*. The believer (and the grateful worshipper of verse 2) is precisely the one who refuses to become a *shāniʾ*; the adversary is the one who became nothing else.

There is even a quiet denial in the word-choice. A *khaṣm*, a disputant, would have a **case** — a two-sided contention granting the opponent some standing. By declining *khaṣm* and choosing *shāniʾ*, the surah grants the man **no claim at all**: he is not a party with an argument but simply a heart full of hatred — and hatred has no case to make. The surah refuses to dignify the animus with a dispute [INFERENCE].

We mark the confidence. **[MEASURED]:** the contrasting datasets — *ʿaduww* (106×, external hostility: Satan, the disbelievers, the war-foe), *khaṣm* (18×, the litigant/disputant: 38:21–22; 22:19; 36:77), *shāniʾ* (3×, the hater); that the two non-108 uses are the **abstract noun** *shanaʾān* ("hatred") while only 108:3 has the **participle** *shāniʾ* ("the hater") with the possessive "your" — a form unique to this verse; and that those two uses (5:2; 5:8) both concern *hatred as a force that corrupts its bearer*. **[INFERRED]:** that *shāniʾ* is chosen to name the **inner cause** (hatred) of the *abtar*-fate, as the **affective opposite** of verse 2's grateful turning, and that 5:8's principle (hatred derails the hater) explains the self-severance of 108:3 (strong cross-reference, since those are the *only* other occurrences of the very word); and the finer point that *shāniʾ* (vs *khaṣm*) denies the man any standing (an inference from the role-difference, slightly softer). The core result is firm: the surah names the adversary by the **disease in his heart**, because it is that disease — not his external opposition — that cuts him off.
