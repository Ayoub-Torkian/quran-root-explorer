# Abstract

Sūrat al-Kawthar is the shortest chapter of the Qur'an — three verses, ten words, sealed by a single rhyme. It is also, for its length, the most lexically singular passage in the entire book. This study reads the surah using one rule and no other: *al-Qur'ān yufassiru baʿḍuhu baʿḍan* — "the Qur'an explains itself." Nothing outside the text is admitted as evidence. Working from the consonantal skeleton (rasm) of all 6,236 verses, we show three things. First, two of the surah's seven content roots — *naḥr* ("sacrifice", verse 2) and *abtar* ("cut off", verse 3) — occur **only here** in the whole Qur'an; they are *hapax legomena*, words with no second home. A third, *kawthar* itself, is a one-of-a-kind word built on an otherwise common root. Second, a self-interpreting text cannot define a word from a single appearance — there is nothing to compare it to — so the Qur'an supplies meaning by three internal routes: the **root family** (the 167 verses that share *kawthar*'s root), the **semantic field** (the wider vocabulary of sacrifice and of severance), and the **surah's own architecture**. Third, that architecture is a tight antithesis — abundance and continuation against severance and no-issue — framed by an emphatic ring (*innā … inna*), stitched by a second-person thread ("you / your") that runs through every verse, and closed by a reversal that returns an insult to its sender. The whole reading is recovered without recourse to the disputed "occasion of revelation" report that classical and modern commentary has leaned on. We present nine figures and three tables, survey how the surah has been read from al-Ṭabarī to Cuypers and Farrin, and argue that the computer's proper role here is not to *generate* meaning but to make a close human reading **complete and honest** — to bring the whole corpus to bear on three short verses.

**Keywords:** Sūrat al-Kawthar; tafsīr al-Qur'ān bi'l-Qur'ān; hapax legomenon; intratextuality; Qur'anic rhetoric; corpus linguistics; ring composition.

# 1. Introduction

Imagine you are handed a single photograph of a stranger and asked to describe their character. One picture is not much to go on. But now imagine you are also handed a thick family album — parents, siblings, cousins, the family at work and at rest across decades. Suddenly the single photograph speaks: you read the stranger through the family resemblance. This is, almost exactly, the problem the Qur'an sets itself in Sūrat al-Kawthar, and the way it solves it.

The surah is famous for being the shortest in the book. In the space a modern reader crosses in a single breath, it makes a complete movement: a gift is announced, a response is commanded, and a verdict is delivered on an enemy. Generations of readers have felt that the chapter is doing something remarkable with very little material. What has been harder to show — rather than merely assert — is *how* it does so, and *what the words themselves contribute* before any outside story is brought in to explain them.

This paper takes a deliberately strict path. We adopt a single interpretive law, classical in its pedigree and exacting in its demands: **the Qur'an is explained by the Qur'an** (*al-Qur'ān yufassiru baʿḍuhu baʿḍan*). Under this rule, the only admissible evidence for what a Qur'anic word means is other Qur'anic usage. Reports about the circumstances of a verse's revelation, traditions of interpretation, and the findings of history and philology are all interesting and will be surveyed — but none of them is allowed to *settle* a meaning. The text is treated the way a careful editor treats a self-contained system: every term must be cashed out in the system's own currency.

We also adopt a particular *substrate*. The object of analysis is the **rasm**, the bare consonantal skeleton of the text — the letters without the later vowel-marks and reading-signs. We do this not to diminish the recited tradition but because the rasm is the layer common to the whole transmission; the dots and vowels, however venerable, are a human aid added on top, and we keep our evidence to the shared bedrock. Practically, this means our unit of measurement is the **root**: the two-, three-, or four-consonant core from which Arabic words are spun. When we say a word "occurs *N* times," we mean its root does, counted across all 6,236 verses of the corpus.

The questions we ask are simple to state. (1) How *rare* are the words of al-Kawthar, measured against the whole book? (2) When a word appears only once, how can a self-interpreting text possibly define it? (3) Does the surah's meaning hold together from the inside — from its roots, its grammar, and its sound — without importing an external backstory? (4) What, then, is the surah *about*, read on its own terms?

Our answers, in brief: the surah is lexically extraordinary for its size; the Qur'an defines its rare words by family resemblance and by the company those words keep elsewhere; the surah coheres beautifully from the inside; and what it is about is the difference between two kinds of "muchness" and two kinds of legacy — a difference the text draws with its own vocabulary and then dramatizes in a single, decisive reversal.

A word on method and honesty, since both are easy to abuse. Throughout, we tag every claim. A claim marked **[TEXT]** is something the Qur'an itself says or displays. A claim marked **[REPORT]** is an external transmission — an occasion-of-revelation narrative, a traditional gloss — which we treat as corroborative at most and never as fact. A claim marked **[INFERENCE]** is our own reasoning. Separately, for the quantitative findings, **[MEASURED]** marks a number actually produced by counting the corpus, while **[INFERRED]** marks our interpretation of what that number means. The distinction matters because the recurring danger in this kind of work is not bad arithmetic but over-reading: letting an interpretation borrow the authority of a measurement. We have tried to keep the two visibly apart.

# 2. The text

The surah, in the canonical order, reads as follows. (Arabic verses are quoted for reference and are not counted toward the study's word total.)

> ١ إِنَّا أَعْطَيْنَاكَ الْكَوْثَرَ
> ٢ فَصَلِّ لِرَبِّكَ وَانْحَرْ
> ٣ إِنَّ شَانِئَكَ هُوَ الْأَبْتَرُ

In transliteration: *(1) innā aʿṭaynāka l-kawthar; (2) fa-ṣalli li-rabbika wa-nḥar; (3) inna shāni'aka huwa l-abtar.*

A plain rendering: *(1) "Indeed We have given you the abundance; (2) so pray to your Lord and sacrifice; (3) indeed it is your hater who is the one cut off."* [TEXT]

Three verses; on the rasm, ten words. Seven of those words carry a lexical root (the others are particles and the article): **ʿaṭā** "to give", **kawthar** built on the root *k–th–r* "to be many / abundant", **ṣalāt** "prayer", **rabb** "Lord", **naḥr** "sacrificial slaughter", **shāni'** "hater", and **abtar** "cut off, without issue." Everything that follows grows from reading these seven roots across the entire Qur'an, and from looking carefully at how the three verses are built.

# 3. How the surah has been read: a literature review

Before turning to the corpus, it is worth laying out — fairly and in some detail — how this small surah has been interpreted, because the history of its reading is also a history of a methodological temptation. We group the scholarship into three streams: classical exegesis, the modern literary-structural turn, and the theory of Qur'anic self-interpretation itself. Throughout this section, claims attributed to commentators are **[REPORT]**: we are reporting positions, not endorsing them as evidence for the text's meaning.

## 3.1 Classical exegesis (tafsīr)

The classical commentators faced two genuine puzzles in this surah, and a third that they largely created for themselves.

The first puzzle is the word **kawthar**. It is a striking form — an intensive pattern on the root of "muchness" — and it occurs nowhere else. Faced with a one-off word, the tradition generated an abundance of readings to match its meaning. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī (d. 1210), in his vast *Mafātīḥ al-ghayb*, famously catalogues a long series of opinions on what *kawthar* denotes; later surveys count on the order of two dozen distinct views, from "a river in Paradise," to "abundant good" (*al-khayr al-kathīr*), to prophethood, the Qur'an itself, the multitude of the Prophet's followers, or his progeny [REPORT]. Al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), the dean of early exegetes, leans toward "the abundant good" while recording the Paradise-river view; al-Zamakhsharī (d. 1144) and al-Qurṭubī (d. 1273) rehearse and weigh the options; Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373) collects the relevant traditions [REPORT]. The very length of these lists is telling: when a word has no second occurrence to anchor it, interpretation tends to expand to fill the vacuum. This is precisely the gap a self-interpreting method is designed to close — not by adding a twenty-seventh opinion, but by asking what the *root* does everywhere else.

The second puzzle is the word **abtar**, "cut off." Here al-Ṭabarī offers a careful linguistic analysis: *al-abtar* is one severed from posterity, from remembrance, and from every form of good — the superlative of a man with no tail, no continuation, no sequel [REPORT]. The exegetes uniformly grasp the rhetorical point, sharpened by commentators across the tradition: the surah sets the **abundance** given to the Prophet against the **severance** assigned to his enemy; *kawthar* and *abtar* are the two poles of one antithesis [REPORT]. This insight is correct and important, and our corpus reading will confirm and quantify it from the inside.

The third matter is the **occasion of revelation** (*sabab al-nuzūl*). A well-known body of reports holds that the surah answered a specific taunt: that when the Prophet's sons died in infancy, an opponent of Quraysh — most often named as al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wā'il — mocked him as *abtar*, "cut off," a man whose name would die with him; the surah is then read as the divine rebuttal, reassigning the epithet to the mocker [REPORT]. We flag this report with care, for three reasons. First, the naming is *not stable*: different transmissions assign the taunt to different figures (al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wā'il, Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf, Abū Jahl, ʿUqba, and others), which is the signature of a story reconstructed around a verse rather than securely remembered alongside it. Second, by the rule we have adopted, an occasion-report is **[REPORT]**, not **[TEXT]**: corroborative at best, never probative. Third — and this is the substantive point of the present study — the surah's meaning does **not require** the report. If *abtar* can be read off the text's own vocabulary, and if the antithesis is visible in the architecture, then the backstory becomes an optional illustration, not a load-bearing beam. Much classical (and, as we will see, modern) commentary treats it as load-bearing. We will show it need not be.

## 3.2 The modern literary-structural turn

A second stream of scholarship, largely of the last half-century, studies the Qur'an's *composition* — the architecture of its surahs — rather than the lexical meaning of individual words. Amīn Aḥsan Iṣlāḥī and, in English, Mustansir Mir revived the older notion of *naẓm* (coherent order), arguing that surahs are structured wholes rather than loose collections [REPORT]. Angelika Neuwirth's foundational studies of the Meccan surahs drew attention to their verse-divisions and rhyme (*fawāṣil*) as compositional features [REPORT]. Most ambitiously, Michel Cuypers and Raymond Farrin have applied the tools of "Semitic rhetoric" — parallelism, mirror composition, and concentric (ring) composition of the form ABC…C′B′A′ — to argue that Qur'anic texts are organized symmetrically around central pivots [REPORT].

This work is illuminating and, for a chapter as compact as al-Kawthar, directly relevant: the surah's symmetry is exactly the kind of feature these scholars taught readers to see. But the approach has also drawn sharp and, in our view, fair criticism. Nicolai Sinai, in a well-known review essay ("Going Round in Circles," *Journal of Qur'anic Studies*, 2017), argues that Cuypers and Farrin overplay their hand — that ring structures, sought everywhere, are found everywhere, and that the method can underweight plainer features such as rhyme [REPORT]. The deeper limitation, for our purposes, is different: structural analysis tends to describe *form* while leaving the **lexical and semantic substrate** — what the actual roots mean and how often they occur — out of the measurement. A diagram of a surah's symmetry is a claim about shape; it does not, by itself, tell you why *these* words sit at the corners. Our study tries to join the two: to show that the architecture and the vocabulary are saying the same thing, and to back both with counts.

## 3.3 The theory of Qur'anic self-interpretation

The method we use is not novel; it is one of the oldest principles of Qur'anic exegesis, given its classic formulation by Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328) in his *Muqaddima fī uṣūl al-tafsīr*: the soundest interpretation is the Qur'an by the Qur'an, "for what is stated briefly in one place is expanded in another, and what is summarized in one place is explained elsewhere" [REPORT]. Modern scholarship (for instance, the analysis of *tafsīr al-Qur'ān bi'l-Qur'ān* in the *Journal of Scriptural Reasoning*, 2018) has revisited the principle and its operational meaning: to grasp a term left undefined in one passage, one searches the rest of the text for its clarification [REPORT]. A frequently cited example is, fittingly, our own root: the word *takāthur* ("rivalry for increase") that opens Sūrat al-Takāthur (102) is illuminated by 57:20, where the same root appears in a fuller, explicitly evaluated context.

What the present study adds to this venerable principle is **scale and discipline**. Classical self-interpretation proceeded from a scholar's memory of parallels; it was powerful but unsystematic, and it could not easily *prove* that it had considered every occurrence. A corpus lets us do exactly that: gather **all** the places a root appears, characterize the company it keeps, and report the distribution rather than a curated handful of parallels. The computer does not interpret; it guarantees coverage. It is the difference between a scholar who recalls "several" relevant verses and an apparatus that can state, and display, that a root occurs in 162 verses across 51 surahs and show how they divide. With that in hand, we turn to the surah itself.
