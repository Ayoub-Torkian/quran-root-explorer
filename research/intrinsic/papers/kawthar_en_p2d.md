## 5.12 Given and earned: the gift and its response

The choice of *aʿṭā* — "a gift," not a wage — opens a larger question the surah quietly answers: in the Qur'an, what is **given** and what is **earned**? The text keeps two vocabularies, and the difference between them is one of its deepest structural features. Reading both across the corpus shows that *al-Kawthar* sits squarely on the "given" side, and that the surah then models the proper human response in the "earned" register.

**The "given" register.** Grace, in the Qur'an, is bestowed by God's will and is **not metered to desert.** Its signature phrases recur: God "gives wisdom *to whomever He wishes*" (2:269); bounty "is in God's hand; He gives it *to whomever He wishes*" (3:73; 62:4, "Allah is the dispenser of *great grace*"); He provides and bestows "*without reckoning*" (*bi-ghayri ḥisāb*, 38:39; cf. 24:38) [TEXT]. The gift comes first and exceeds measure; it is the initiative of the Giver, not the wage of the receiver.

**The "earned" register.** Against this stands an equally insistent vocabulary of **acquisition and its exact recompense.** "Every soul is hostage to *what it has earned*" (74:38); "whatever good it earns is to its own benefit, and whatever evil it earns is to its own detriment" (2:286); "*nothing belongs to man except what he strives for*" (53:39); "whoever does *an atom's weight* of good will see it, and whoever does *an atom's weight* of evil will see it" (99:7–8) [TEXT]. Here the logic is precise proportion: you reap what you sow, down to the atom. The very last word of the human story, in 84:6, is labour: "O man, you are *toiling* toward your Lord, *laboriously*."

**How the two relate.** They are not rivals but an **ordered pair**. Grace comes first and grounds; deeds respond and are recompensed; and then grace returns to **exceed** the desert it recompenses — the good deed is "multiplied seven hundredfold" (2:261), "doubled, with a great reward from Himself" (4:40), "their full reward, and an increase from His bounty" (35:30) [TEXT]. Even the earned is finally crowned by gift. Guidance shows the loop turning in both directions at once: "those who *strive* in Us, *We shall guide* them" (29:69) — the human strives, and the guidance is given [TEXT]. The Qur'an thus refuses both pure merit (you cannot simply earn your standing; grace is needed and overflows) and pure passivity (deeds are required and weighed). It holds gift and effort together, in that order.

**Al-Kawthar in miniature.** The surah is a compressed model of exactly this order. Verse 1 is **pure gift**: *al-Kawthar* is *given* (*aʿṭaynāka*), abundant, unearned, simply bestowed "to you." Verse 2 is the **response in the earned register** — prayer and sacrifice, the works of gratitude — and the grammar makes the relationship explicit: the verse opens with *fa-* ("**so** / therefore"): *fa-ṣalli* [TEXT]. Worship is the **consequence** of the gift, not its purchase price; one prays *because* one has been given, not *in order to be* given. The gift precedes and motivates the work. And verse 3 supplies the dark mirror: the **hater** is *al-abtar*, the cut-off one — his severance bound, by the surah's own juxtaposition, to his chosen enmity. So the chapter maps the whole economy onto two figures: **abundance is given** (to the one who receives and gives thanks), while **severance is, in effect, earned** (by the one whose stance is hatred) [INFERENCE on the juxtaposition of *shāniʾ* and *abtar*].

This also sharpens the two-faces reading of *kawthar* from §5.2. The blameworthy twin, *takāthur*, is abundance one competes and *piles up* for oneself — a corrupt form of acquisition, "earning" turned to greed. *Kawthar* is its opposite precisely as **gift**: not seized or amassed, but received from the Lord and returned to Him in worship. The difference between the two "abundances" is, at bottom, the difference between grasping and being given [INFERENCE].

### 5.12.1 The mechanism: multiplied, asymmetric, and *brought* (not merely done)

The "grace exceeds desert" claim is not vague; the Qur'an states the **exchange rate**, and it is deeply asymmetric. "Whoever **comes with** a good deed shall have **ten times** its like; and whoever **comes with** a bad deed shall be requited **only with its like**" (6:160) [TEXT]. Good is multiplied at least tenfold; evil is capped at exactly one-to-one — *lā yujzā illā mithlahā*, "repaid only by its equal." The ledger is tilted, by design, toward mercy: the good is amplified, the harm contained (and, elsewhere, forgivable). Parallel verses raise the good side even higher — "whoever comes with a good deed shall have **better than it**" (27:89; 28:84), with the same one-to-one cap reaffirmed for evil [TEXT].

And the multiplication can be extravagant. The famous parable of 2:261 sets the rate at **seven hundred**: spending in God's way "is like a grain that grows **seven ears** (*sabʿ sanābil*), in **every ear a hundred grains** (*miʾat ḥabba*); and God **multiplies** (*yuḍāʿif*) for whom He wills" — sealing the verse with the divine names **"All-embracing, All-knowing" (*wāsiʿ ʿalīm*)** [TEXT]. The arithmetic of the verse is worth following, because it deliberately **runs off the end of the number line**. One grain becomes seven ears; each ear, a hundred grains — seven hundred. Then comes *yuḍāʿif*, "He multiplies": the verb is not "adds" but doubles-and-more, so the seven hundred is at the least carried to fourteen hundred, and — since *yuḍāʿif* means manifold increase, not a fixed times-two — beyond. Then comes *wāsiʿ*, "All-embracing / boundless": and with that word the ceiling is removed altogether. **No one knows the upper figure**, because the Giver's capacity has no edge. Yet the verse does not end on *wāsiʿ* alone; it adds *ʿalīm*, "All-knowing." The boundlessness is **not careless excess**: the same God who gives without limit gives with perfect knowledge — He knows exactly what He does. The two names together say abundance *and* wisdom: limitless in scope, precise in purpose [INFERENCE on the force of the paired names]. This is the very signature of *kawthar* — not a heap of "a lot," but a vastness (*wāsiʿ*) that is also ordered and known (*ʿalīm*). The verb of multiplication, *yuḍāʿif* (root *ḍ-ʿ-f*), recurs 52 times; "All-embracing" (*wāsiʿ*, root *w-s-ʿ*) appears 32 times and is paired again and again with **grace** (*faḍl*) and forgiveness (2:268; 3:73; 24:32) [MEASURED]. The point for us is striking: this whole economy is described in **the very vocabulary of *kawthar***. When 2:245 promises that a good loan to God is multiplied "**manifold**," the word it chooses is *aḍʿāfan **kathīra*** — built on *k-th-r*, the root of *kawthar* itself [TEXT]. The reward-economy is, lexically and conceptually, *kawthar*-shaped: abundance, vastness (*wāsiʿ*), multiplication without stint. The gift of verse 1 is one instance of a God whose entire mode of recompense is super-abundant.

There is, finally, a fine grammatical point that deepens this. The reward verses do not say "whoever **does** (*faʿala*) a good deed" but "whoever **comes / brings** (*jāʾa bi-*) a good deed." *Jāʾa bi-* is the language of **arriving bearing something** — presenting a good one *carries*, a settled possession one brings forward (supremely, on the Day one is raised). The nuance, fairly read, is **continuity rather than the transient**: not a flickering, occasional act that flares and fades, but a sustained good that endures and is **brought intact** to be multiplied — taken at tenfold, not eroded or "demoted" by being offset at parity against one's lapses (the lapse, after all, is capped at its own single weight) [INFERENCE]. This is the same opposition the surah turns on: *kawthar* is the **continuing, brought-forward** abundance; *al-abtar* is the cut-off, the line that does not arrive. The believer who *comes bearing* good participates, in miniature, in the logic of *kawthar*; the *abtar* is precisely the one with nothing that continues.

This yields the sharpest formulation of the whole section: **even in the register of "earning," the good is never repaid one-to-one.** The dividing line between "given" and "earned" is itself asymmetric. For the good deed there is, strictly speaking, *no pure earning at all* — the recompense always exceeds the merit (tenfold, seven-hundredfold, "better than it," "doubled with a great reward from Himself," 4:40); grace overflows into the earned column and inflates it. Strict, proportionate desert — the exact wage for the exact work — is reserved for **evil alone** (*lā yujzā illā mithlahā*), and even that is open to pardon. So the Qur'an's economy is not half-grace and half-merit; it is grace that *initiates* (the unearned gift), grace that *responds* (the multiplied reward of works), and bare justice only on the side of harm. Read back into the surah: verse 1 (the given *kawthar*) and the worship of verse 2 (the "earned" response, whose reward will itself be multiplied) are **not two economies but one** — a single overflowing generosity — while only verse 3, the hater's severance, falls under plain desert. The abundance is grace upon grace; the cutting-off is justice [INFERENCE built on the [MEASURED] 6:160 asymmetry].

### 5.12.2 God the buyer: a trade in which the capital was a gift

The Qur'an presses the point further with a daring image: God as a **merchant who buys**. "Indeed God has **bought** (*ishtarā*) from the believers their **souls** and their **wealth**, that theirs shall be the Garden" (9:111) — and the verse calls it a *bayʿ*, a "bargain," telling them to "**rejoice in the bargain you have made**: that is the **supreme triumph**" [TEXT]. The mirror from the human side: "among people is he who **sells** his soul seeking God's pleasure" (2:207). It is named outright a **commerce** (*tijāra*) — "shall I show you a trade that will save you from a painful punishment?" (61:10) [TEXT].

Three features make this the capstone of the given/earned argument. First, the **return vastly exceeds the price**: the payment for one's perishable self and wealth is Paradise, and the parallel "loan" image says it plainly — "who will **lend** God a goodly loan, that He may **multiply** it for him **manifold** (*aḍʿāfan kathīra*)?" (2:245; cf. 57:11; 64:17), once again reaching for *kawthar*'s own root. Second, the commerce is **everlasting** — "they hope for a **commerce that will never perish**" (*tijāra lan **tabūr***, 35:29). That phrase is a lexical thunderclap for our surah: *tabūr* is from *bawār*, "to perish," a member of the very **severance field** that defines *al-abtar* (§5.4). The believer's trade is, in so many words, the *un-cut-off*, *never-perishing* deal — the exact opposite of being *abtar*. Third — and this is the profundity the trade conceals — **what God buys was His own gift to begin with.** Those who trade "spend **out of what We have provided them**" (*mimmā razaqnāhum*, 35:29); God "**created you and whatever you make**" (37:96); "to God belongs all that is in the heavens and the earth" (2:284). The seller's entire capital — his life, his wealth, even his deeds — was *given* him by the Buyer. So God purchases what already belongs to Him, and pays for it with the everlasting Garden, multiplied. The "profit," tellingly, is named only once in the whole Qur'an, and there it is a *failure* — the deniers "bought error for guidance, so their commerce **did not profit**" (2:16) [TEXT/MEASURED: *ribḥ* is a hapax]. The believer's trade is the one that profits.

This dissolves any residual notion of "earning." The commerce with God is grace clothed as exchange: the capital is gift, the buy-back is grace, the return is super-abundant and unperishing. Read into al-Kawthar, the surah is itself this trade in miniature: God **gives** the abundance (verse 1), the servant **spends it back** in prayer and **sacrifice** (verse 2 — *naḥr* is precisely returning to God a portion of what He gave, its substance "your piety," 22:37), and what comes back is the never-perishing good. The believer's "commerce that does not perish" is *kawthar*; the hater's lot is to *perish* — *abtar*, *bā'ir*. The two halves of the surah's spine turn out to be the two outcomes of the one divine trade [INFERENCE].

We mark the confidence. **[MEASURED]:** the two vocabularies and their sizes; the signature phrases of each ("to whom He wills," "without reckoning"; "only what he strove for," "atom's weight"); the reward-multiplying verses; the explicit good-vs-evil asymmetry of 6:160 (ten-to-one vs one-to-one); the 700-fold parable of 2:261 sealed by *wāsiʿ ʿalīm*; the use of the *kathīr* root for the multiplied reward in 2:245; the *jāʾa bi-* ("comes with") wording shared by 6:160, 27:89 and 28:84; and the *fa-* that opens 108:2. **[INFERRED]:** the synthesis that grace initiates and exceeds while deeds respond — a reading of the assembled verses (widely recognized, securely grounded, but a theological synthesis, not a single measured fact); and the mapping of "earned severance" onto the hater, which is the softest link here (the text juxtaposes *hater* and *cut-off* rather than stating a recompense outright). The *fa-* of consequence in verse 2, by contrast, is a hard textual fact and carries the core point: in al-Kawthar, the gift comes first, and worship follows from it.
