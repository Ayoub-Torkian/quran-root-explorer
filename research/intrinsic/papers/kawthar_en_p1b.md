## 3.4 What *is* al-Kawthar? Sunni and Shia referents — and the Fāṭima reading

Because *kawthar* is a unique word (§5.1), the question "what does it *refer* to?" has been answered in markedly different ways, and the two great interpretive traditions diverge in an instructive direction. We survey both as **[REPORT]** — positions held, not evidence for the text's sense — and note where each bears on our corpus reading.

**The Sunni mainstream: abundant good, and the river of Paradise.** The earliest and most widely transmitted Sunni gloss, traced to Ibn ʿAbbās and recorded by al-Ṭabarī, reads *al-kawthar* as *al-khayr al-kathīr* — "the abundant good," a deliberately open sense that the commentators say *includes* the more specific referents [REPORT]. Alongside it stands a hadith-anchored identification, reported by Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī, Ibn Mājah and others and treated as sound in the Sunni tradition: *al-Kawthar* is "a **river in Paradise**, its banks of gold, its water whiter than milk and sweeter than honey," together with the *ḥawḍ*, the Prophet's **fountain** at which his community will drink on the Day of Gathering [REPORT]. Ibn Kathīr gathers these and treats "abundant good" as the comprehensive meaning, the river being one of its contents. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, as noted (§3.1), catalogues a long list of further candidate referents. On progeny, the Sunni commentaries generally read the surah as consoling the Prophet against the taunt of childlessness and affirming that his honour and following would abound; they acknowledge that his bloodline in fact continued through his daughter Fāṭima — but they do **not** identify *kawthar itself* with Fāṭima. For the Sunni mainstream, *kawthar* is the abundant good (and the Paradisal river); the continuing progeny is one fruit of that abundance, not the word's referent.

**The Shia reading: kawthar exemplified in Fāṭima.** The major Shia exegetes begin from the same lexical base — *kawthar* as *al-khayr al-kathīr*, "abundant good without limit" — but press toward a concrete manifestation. Muḥammad Ḥusayn Ṭabāṭabāʾī, in *al-Mīzān fī tafsīr al-Qurʾān*, reads the surah's logic tightly: the opponent's jibe was *abtar*, "cut off [without posterity]"; the divine answer is *kawthar*, "abundance"; and the abundance that concretely rebuts the jibe is the Prophet's **enduring progeny**, which descends **through Fāṭima** alone [REPORT]. ʿAbdullāh Javādī Āmolī's *Tasnīm* — a contemporary commentary that, by its own account, follows al-Mīzān's method of *interpreting the Qurʾān by the Qurʾān* (the very method of the present study) — develops the same line: among the clearest instances of the "abundant good" is the person of Fāṭima al-Zahrāʾ, from whom the Prophet's descendants multiplied across the world [REPORT]. Nāṣir Makārim Shīrāzī (in his commentary and in *Fāṭima al-Zahrāʾ in the Noble Qurʾān*) makes the identification explicit: *kawthar* is exemplified in Fāṭima, since the Prophet's line — and with it the Imams — continues through her, so that it is the mocker, not the Prophet, who proves *abtar* [REPORT]. In this reading *kawthar* is at once the abundant good *and*, paradigmatically, the daughter through whom that good endures.

**Why this matters for our study — and a caution against ourselves.** Two points follow, one methodological and one self-critical. Methodologically, it is striking that the two commentaries we lean on for the Shia view, *al-Mīzān* and *Tasnīm*, are precisely the classic exemplars of *tafsīr al-Qurʾān bi'l-Qurʾān* — the discipline this paper tries to execute at corpus scale; our method is their method, made exhaustive by counting. Self-critically, the Fāṭima reading exposes a tension in our own §5.11. There we argued that the surah, by choosing the open gift-verb *aʿṭā* and **naming no offspring**, *relocates* "continuation" away from biological lineage. The Shia reading answers the *abtar* taunt **with lineage** — continuation precisely *as* progeny, through Fāṭima. Both cannot be pressed to the hilt at once. The honest resolution, which we adopt, is that our corpus method fixes only the **sense** of *kawthar* — abundant good, marked as continuing and God-given (§5.2, §5.14) — while deliberately leaving its **referent open**; the traditions then supply candidate referents (the river and abundant good in the Sunni mainstream; Fāṭima and the enduring progeny in the Shia reading). Our §5.11 therefore overstated matters if it implied the text *excludes* a progeny referent; more carefully, the text does not *name* progeny, leaving room for the very referent the Shia exegetes supply. We return to this, with the other objections, in the dedicated counter-reading section (§7).
